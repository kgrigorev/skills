## 1. What STE is

- STE is a controlled natural language and an international standard to write technical documentation.
- It has two parts. Part 1 is the writing rules (grammar and style). Part 2 is the controlled dictionary (the words a writer can use).
- Purpose: tell technical writers how to write technical text in a clear, simple and unambiguous way that readers worldwide find easy to understand.
- Reason: English is the international language of science, technology and human relations, but it is often not the native language of the readers of technical documentation. Many readers have a limited knowledge of English. The large number of meanings and synonyms of many English words, and a complex sentence structure, can cause confusion. In aerospace, readers must understand maintenance and operation documentation correctly, to make sure that systems operate safely and correctly and to protect human lives.
- It was made for aerospace maintenance documentation. Other industries now use it beyond that original purpose, and interest has also grown in language services, professional translation and interpreting, and in the academic world. It was developed for technical documentation only. Its rules and dictionary can also help oral communication in meetings and presentations.
- One main aim is easier translation (by translators, neural machine translation engines and Large Language Models). If the permitted words, their meanings and the types of sentence construction are controlled, the difference between texts decreases to a minimum.
- The writing rules (part 1) have 9 sections with 53 rules. Most of the content of the rules is itself written in STE. The spelling and punctuation obey American English rules.

### What STE does not do

- It is not a treatise on English or on technical writing. Grammar and linguistics are not fully covered. They are in other reference books.
- It is not an English course book. It helps the writer give complex information in a form that is easy to understand. Writing clearly is a complex task, and a high level of fluency in English is needed to write STE correctly.
- It has no rules for abbreviations. Industries, companies and projects can use different abbreviations, so rules are not necessary.
- It does not regulate text formatting (for example typeface, numbering, lettering). It regulates the way to express the content. Usually, the applicable specifications for technical publications, style guides and other official directives include these requirements.
- It does not regulate units of measurement. There are different methods to show them in a technical text. Projects and companies must decide which one to use. Usually, the applicable specifications for technical publications, style guides and other official directives include these requirements.
- It cannot be used alone. Use it together with the applicable specifications for technical publications, style guides and official directives. A high standard of professionalism is necessary to use STE correctly.
- The disclaimer (p. 2) says the document, including its writing rules, dictionary entries and related examples, offers only recommendations and definitions to assist technical communication. It is not intended to create legal obligations or confer legal rights. No part of it should be interpreted as having legal significance or implications, regardless of the context in which it is used.

### Origin, maintenance, training and distribution

- Late 1970s: the Association of European Airlines (AEA) asked the European Association of Aerospace Industries (AECMA, now ASD) to investigate the readability of maintenance documentation in the civil aviation industry and to find a solution to simplify its language. AECMA asked the Aerospace Industries Association (AIA) of America to help. Two project groups from AECMA and AIA explored existing controlled natural languages and researched texts in many maintenance manuals. The results confirmed that a simplified language for aerospace was necessary.
- 30.06.1983, Amsterdam: the AECMA Simplified English Working Group was founded and the AECMA Simplified English project started. The result was the AECMA Simplified English Guide (first released in 1986), which became ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English in 2005.
- 1987: the Air Transport Association of America (ATA) included the requirement to write in STE in its ATA100 specification for technical publications. The name ASD-STE100 was assigned as a tribute to the name of the ATA100 specification.

### Guide to the writing rules (as given in the general introduction)

- On the first page of each section is the list of all rules of that section. Then there are explanations and examples for each rule.
- After the explanation of a rule, non-STE and STE examples are in different typefaces and colors. The document shows the pattern like this:
  - Non-STE: Make sure that the valve is operable.
  - STE: Make sure that the valve can operate.
- General examples, not related to STE, are in another typeface and color, for example: "An opening door can be dangerous."
- When you read the writing rules, always refer to the dictionary (part 2). Its introduction has all the information needed to use the dictionary correctly.
- Help: for some rules in part 1 (and some dictionary words), a graphic symbol (light bulb) with related text gives more information or examples about the applicable rule. This symbol is called "help". The dictionary introduction explains the types of help (see section 3).

### Vocabulary basics (from the general introduction)

- The dictionary words are the words most frequently used in technical writing. They were selected because they were simple and easy to recognize.
- In general, each word has only one meaning and is approved with only one part of speech. Example: "to fall" means "to move down by the force of gravity", and not "to decrease".
- When English has synonyms, STE picks one and leaves out the others. Example: STE uses "start" instead of "begin", "commence", "initiate" or "originate".
- Approved meanings and spelling are based on American English and the Merriam-Webster's dictionary (the reference list names Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary, 11th ed.). The spelling and punctuation of the rules and the dictionary examples obey American English rules.
- Writers use the approved words in the dictionary as a core vocabulary. They can also use noun terms and verb terms that apply to their companies, industries or subject fields. STE identifies these terms as "technical nouns" and "technical verbs" and gives the necessary rules to use them correctly (the Subject-to-rule index names rules 1.1 and 1.5 to 1.13 for technical nouns, and 1.1, 1.12 and 1.13 for technical verbs).
- The term "technical noun" fully replaces the old term "technical name" in Issue 9.

## 3. How to read the dictionary (Part 2)

### Counts and content

- 875 approved words, with examples of how to use each.
- 1274 not-approved words (a "selection"). Each has one or more approved alternatives and an example. The example shows a word-for-word replacement or a different sentence construction.
- The text does not say how the 875 and 1274 are counted (per entry, per meaning, or per part of speech). Do not invent a counting rule.
- The dictionary has no technical nouns or technical verbs as entries. Some appear as alternatives to words that are not approved, marked (TN) for technical nouns and (TV) for technical verbs.
- No entry gives antonyms (opposite meanings) for the listed word.
- Spelling is American English (Merriam-Webster). Punctuation in examples is American English.
- The first column never lists a technical noun or verb. A not-approved word in the dictionary can still be a technical noun or verb in some contexts (rule 1.5 for nouns, rule 1.12 for verbs).
- Words and the flowchart: the dictionary lists only a selection of not-approved words. The document says: "If a word is not approved in the dictionary, do not use it." Its page "How to select words correctly" (p. 2-0-16) says that STE has a restricted dictionary, so it is not possible to use all the words that you want, and that if you are not sure about a word you want to use, you refer to a flowchart. The flowchart is a graphic, so its text is not in the extracted pages. The pages do not say how to decide a word that is in the dictionary neither as approved nor as not approved.

### Four columns

1. Word (part of speech). The word, in bold typeface, with its part of speech.
2. Approved meaning / ALTERNATIVES. For an approved word: its approved meaning. For a not-approved word: the approved alternatives.
3. STE EXAMPLE. Correct words and constructions.
4. Non-STE example. Words that are not approved and constructions not permitted in STE.

### Column 1

- UPPERCASE = approved, you can use it. lowercase = not approved, you cannot use it. To replace it in a text, you must use a different word or a different sentence construction.
- Examples in the document (word | approved meaning or ALTERNATIVES | STE EXAMPLE | Non-STE example):
  - ABRASIVE (adj) | That can remove material by friction | POLISH THE SURFACE WITH AN ABRASIVE PAPER.
  - AID (n) | Help that is given | IF YOU GET THIS CHEMICAL SOLUTION IN YOUR EYES, GET MEDICAL AID IMMEDIATELY.
  - main (adj) | PRIMARY (adj) | THE PRIMARY CAUSE OF VALVE FAILURE IS CONTAMINATION OF THE FLUID. | The main cause of valve failure is contamination of the fluid.
  - build (v) | ASSEMBLE (v) | ASSEMBLE THE UNIT. | Build the unit.
  - accuracy (n) (the introduction's example of a not-approved entry) | PRECISION (n) | THE PRECISION OF THE ADJUSTMENT CAN CHANGE. | The accuracy of the adjustment can vary.
- The part of speech is in parentheses. Use an approved word only as the specified part of speech. The same spelling can be approved as one part of speech and not as another. Examples from the Highlights: FACE (n) approved but face (v) not approved; FLOW and POINT approved as both (n) and (v); ALTERNATIVE approved as (adj) and as (n); MORE and MOST approved as (adj) and (adv). Approval is per word and part of speech.
- Eight parts of speech: noun (n), verb (v), adjective (adj), adverb (adv), pronoun (pron), article (art), preposition (prep), conjunction (conj).
  - Noun: name of a person, place, object, idea, quality or activity.
  - Verb: a state of being or an action. Tense (present, past, future) tells when.
  - Adjective: gives details about a noun, noun phrase or multi-word noun. It is related to the type, size, color or number of the noun, noun phrase or multi-word noun. Adjectives can be of a qualitative and a quantitative type and can have comparative and superlative forms. Their position in the sentence can be before nouns or after verbs.
  - Adverb: modifies a verb, an adjective or a different adverb. Answers the questions "how," "where," "when," "how often" and "how much."
  - Pronoun: replaces a noun, noun phrase or multi-word noun.
  - Article: tells if a noun phrase is new (indefinite a, an) or already known (definite the).
  - Preposition: shows how a noun, noun phrase, multi-word noun or pronoun relates to other parts of the sentence.
  - Conjunction: a word or phrase that connects words, phrases and clauses.
- Some entries are phrases (for example AS … AS (conj), GO OFF, PUT ON, MAKE SURE, COME ON).

### Forms of approved words

- Nouns: given only in the singular form, but the plural of countable nouns is permitted unless the help says otherwise. Some nouns in the dictionary, although not approved, can be technical nouns in some contexts. Rule 1.5 gives the conditions to use technical nouns correctly. Example: AGENT (n) | One of a group of materials made to do a specified task | DO NOT USE THESE CLEANING AGENTS ON HOT SURFACES.
- Verbs: given in the verb forms that are permitted (refer to part 1, section 3). Do not use verb forms if they are not in the dictionary. The meaning of the verb can be different if it has an object (transitive verb) or no object (intransitive verb). These verbs can have more than one approved meaning and more than one STE example. Some verbs in the dictionary, although not approved, can be technical verbs in some contexts. Rule 1.12 gives the conditions to use technical verbs correctly.
  - Verbs in the dictionary can be regular verbs, irregular verbs, irregular auxiliary verbs or defective modal verbs.
  - Regular verbs: verbs that obey a constant pattern to make the simple past tense and the past participle. Usually they add "-ed" to the base verb form. Do not use the past participle form if it is not in the dictionary. Entry format: base form, third person, simple past tense, past participle. Example: ADAPT (v), ADAPTS, ADAPTED, ADAPTED | To change or adjust to that which is necessary | ADAPT THE PRESSURE CONNECTION TO THE PITOT HEAD. THE SYSTEM INTERFACE CIRCUITS ADAPT TO THE PHYSICAL PROPERTIES OF THE CONNECTED SYSTEMS. (The example entry shows no "-ing" form.)
  - Irregular verbs: verbs that do not obey the standard rules for the simple past tense and the past participle. Example: GIVE (v), GIVES, GAVE, GIVEN | To provide | THIS SECTION GIVES THE CLEANING PROCEDURES FOR THE DISASSEMBLED PARTS.
  - Irregular auxiliary verbs: auxiliary verbs that have unusual forms for tenses. Example: BE (v), IS, WAS (also ARE, WERE), with the help "No other verb forms." (so the forms BEEN and BEING are not given). BE has two approved meanings: 1. To occur, exist | IF THERE IS CORROSION ON THE PUMP VANES, REPLACE THE PUMP. 2. To have a property to be equal to | ACID SOLUTIONS ARE DANGEROUS.
  - Defective modal verbs: modal verbs in which some verb forms are missing.
    - CAN (v), CAN, COULD, with the help "No other verb forms." Meaning: auxiliary modal verb that means to be possible, to be able to, or to be permitted to. Examples: A MIXTURE OF FUEL AND OXYGEN CAN CAUSE AN EXPLOSION. YOU CAN CLEAN THE DRAIN HOLES WITH THE CLEANING TOOL. YOU CAN OPERATE THE VEHICLE AFTER THE INSPECTION IS COMPLETED.

    - WILL (v), with the help "No other verb forms." Meaning: auxiliary modal verb that shows simple future tense. Example: WARNINGS AND CAUTIONS IN THIS MANUAL WILL HELP YOU TO DO THE WORK SAFELY AND CORRECTLY.
- Adjectives: given in their base form, with the comparative and superlative forms in parentheses, for example SLOW (adj) (SLOWER, SLOWEST) | At low speed | MAKE SURE THAT THE MOVEMENT OF THE ELEVATORS IS SLOW. Adjectives that make the comparative and superlative with "more" and "most" do not have these forms in the dictionary, because "more" and "most" are approved words.
- Adverbs: frequently (but not always) you can make an adverb from an adjective by attaching "-ly" at the end. You can make the comparative and superlative forms with "more" and "most". These forms are not in the dictionary. Examples: SLOWLY (adv) | In a slow manner | TURN THE KNOB SLOWLY. briskly (adv) | QUICKLY (adv) | RUB THE SURFACE QUICKLY WITH A SOFT, DRY CLOTH. | Rub the surface briskly with a soft, dry cloth.

### Column 2

- For an approved word: the approved meaning (or definition). The text of these definitions is not in STE. If a meaning is not given in the dictionary, you cannot use the word with a meaning that is not approved. Thus, use a different word that has the meaning you want. Example: BEHIND (prep) | In a position at the rear of | THE PUMP IS INSTALLED BEHIND THE HYDRAULIC MOTOR.
- For a not-approved word: approved alternatives, in uppercase. They are only suggestions to help you. Example: addition (n) | ADD (v) | TO GET THE CORRECT CLEARANCE, ADD SPECIAL SHIMS, AS NECESSARY. | Adjust the clearance by the addition of special shims, as necessary.
- An alternative can have a different part of speech from the word it replaces. Usually the first alternative has the same part of speech. Example: maintain (v) | KEEP (v) | KEEP THE FLUID TEMPERATURE AT 70 °F. | Maintain the fluid temperature at 70 °F. The second alternative, HOLD (v) | TURN THE ROTATING TUBE 75 DEGREES. THEN, HOLD THE TUBE IN THIS POSITION. | Turn the rotating tube 75 degrees. Then maintain the tube in this position. The third alternative, MAINTENANCE (n) | DO MAINTENANCE ON THE WATER SUPPLY SYSTEM. | Maintain the water supply system.
- The first column does not include technical nouns or technical verbs, but the second column can include them as alternatives. The abbreviations TN and TV in parentheses identify them. Example: uncovered (v) | COVER (TN) | DO NOT PUT A COVER ON THE CONTAINER. | Leave the container uncovered.
- An alternative can be a phrase. These alternatives do not include the parts of speech in parentheses. Example: simultaneously (adv) | AT THE SAME TIME | DO THESE TWO STEPS AT THE SAME TIME. | Do these two steps simultaneously.

### Help (graphic symbol with text), four categories

For some words, a graphic symbol and related text tell you to use a different approved alternative or a different sentence construction. This symbol and its text are called "help".

1. Category 1: recommendations that give more information or instructions about how to use the approved word correctly. Example: PUSH (v), PUSHES, PUSHED, PUSHED | 1. To apply a force to something to move it away from the source of the force | PUSH THE INSERT DOWN UNTIL IT TOUCHES THE BOTTOM OF THE HOLE. 2. To move with a force against something | THE SPRING UNIT PUSHES AGAINST THE BRAKE PEDAL. Help: "Use this word together with a preposition or an adverb to show direction."
2. Category 2: some approved words, for example ABOUT (prep), have a restricted meaning. Use the approved alternatives for other meanings. When the help refers to restricted meanings of approved words, the dictionary gives STE and non-STE examples in columns 3 and 4. Example: ABOUT (prep) | Concerned with | FOR DATA ABOUT THE LOCATION OF CIRCUIT BREAKERS, REFER TO THE WIRING LIST. Help: "For other meanings, use:" APPROXIMATELY (adv) | DRAIN APPROXIMATELY 2 LITERS OF FUEL FROM THE TANK. | Drain about 2 liters of fuel from the tank. AROUND (prep) | TURN THE SHAFT AROUND ITS AXIS. | Rotate the shaft about its axis.
3. Category 3: be careful with words that are approved for one context only. Example: SWALLOW (v), SWALLOWS, SWALLOWED, SWALLOWED | To take through the mouth and esophagus into the stomach | IF YOU SWALLOW NITRIC ACID, DO NOT CAUSE VOMITING. GET MEDICAL AID IMMEDIATELY. Help: "Use this word for safety instructions only." The word is only applicable to safety instructions. Do not use it in other contexts.
4. Category 4: important information about the applicable listed words (also those that are not approved). Examples: BE (v), IS, WAS (also ARE, WERE), help "No other verb forms." Also already (adv) | IN PROGRESS (adv) | THE DATABASE SYNCHRONIZATION IS IN PROGRESS. | The database is already synchronizing. NO OTHER | YOU CAN DO THIS REPAIR PROCEDURE ONLY IF THERE IS NO OTHER DAMAGE. | Only do this repair procedure if there is not already damage. Help: "Frequently, an alternative for this word is not necessary." | MAKE SURE THAT THE SOFTWARE IS INSTALLED IN THE REPOSITORY. | Make sure that the software is already installed in the repository.
   - Application to software prose: the already (adv) example shows that "already" is often dropped, not replaced.

### Column 3 (STE EXAMPLE)

- Shows how to use the approved word, how to use the approved alternative (usually a word-for-word replacement) and how to keep the same meaning with a different sentence construction.
- The wording in the STE examples gives recommendations. An example shows only one method to write a text with the approved word or to use alternatives for a word that is not approved. You can frequently use different sentence constructions with other approved words and keep the same meaning.
- Many examples come from the subject field of aircraft maintenance. If you replace the terms in the sentence with other applicable terms, you can use the examples for other subject fields or domains. If you work in a different subject field or domain, you can send a change form with your examples for a possible inclusion in the dictionary.
- Examples in the dictionary are set in uppercase. These pages do not tell writers to write running text in uppercase.
- Examples: A (art) | Function word: indefinite article | A FUEL PUMP IS INSTALLED IN ZONE 10. main (adj) | PRIMARY (adj) | THE PRIMARY CAUSE OF VALVE FAILURE IS CONTAMINATION OF THE FLUID. | The main cause of valve failure is contamination of the fluid. manufacture (v) | MAKE (v) | YOU CAN MAKE THE CLEARING TOOL LOCALLY. | The clearing tool can be manufactured locally.

### Column 4 (Non-STE example)

- Shows how the word that is not approved in STE is used in standard technical English. The examples also help you to understand how you can use the approved alternatives or different sentence constructions to give the same information.
- In a non-STE example you can find more than one word that is not approved or a construction that is not permitted. The STE example shows how to replace these words or use a construction that is permitted in STE.
- For approved words column 4 is empty, unless there is help related to other meanings or restricted meanings. Example: ABOVE (prep) | In (or to) a position farther up than something | LIFT THE CYLINDER ABOVE ITS INSTALLED POSITION. ## 6. Other things a writer must obey from these pages

- Spelling and meanings follow American English and Merriam-Webster. Punctuation follows American English rules.
- Use a word only with its approved part of speech, approved meaning and (where help says so) approved context.
- Use only the verb forms listed. Do not use a form that is not in the dictionary. BE has no other forms. CAN has CAN and COULD only, and COULD is not for possibility. WILL has no other forms.
- Do not use a meaning that the dictionary does not give. Pick another word.
- Where the dictionary suggests an alternative, it is a suggestion. The sentence can be rebuilt with other approved words if the meaning stays the same.
- Technical nouns and technical verbs can be used in addition to the approved words (rules 1.1, 1.5, 1.12). The dictionary marks them (TN) and (TV) when it uses them as alternatives.
- STE does not set abbreviations, formatting or units of measurement. Use the project or company style guide for these.
- Feedback: STE is "a living language". Users send change requests on the change form to the STEMG (see section 1). This does not change what a writer must do.
