### Rule 1.1 Use words that are: - Approved in the dictionary - Technical nouns - Technical verbs.

- STE has a controlled dictionary (part 2) that gives the words most frequently used in technical writing. You can also use words that are not in the dictionary if you can include them in the specified categories of technical nouns and technical verbs.
- "A technical noun is a noun term that refers to a specified concept and is applicable to a subject field. A technical verb is a verb term that also refers to a specified concept or process and is applicable to a subject field."
- The dictionary also gives a selection of words that are not approved, with examples that show how to use alternative words.
- Note: in the context of ISO 1087:2019, "subject fields" refer to specific domains or areas of knowledge that have specialized vocabularies.
- Note: technical nouns and technical verbs are usually included in your company glossary or terminology database. Always refer to these sources, and to the specified rules in this section, for the correct selection of words.
- Examples (document): The word "use" is an approved verb in the dictionary. The word "engine" is a technical noun. The word "ream" is a technical verb.
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) Three sources of words: the dictionary, the project glossary (technical nouns such as "branch", "pipeline", "tenant"), and technical verbs. A word in none of them needs a replacement or a different sentence.

### Rule 1.2 Use approved words from the dictionary only as the specified part of speech.

- In the dictionary, each approved word has a specified part of speech. When you use an approved word, make sure that you use it only as the specified part of speech.
- Some words are approved as more than one part of speech. For example, "clean" is an approved verb and an approved adjective. The position of the word in the sentence shows its function (and its meaning) because verbs and adjectives have different positions.
- If the replacement for a not approved word has the same part of speech, use a word-for-word replacement. If the approved alternative has a different part of speech, use a different sentence construction.
- "When you replace a word, always make sure that the approved alternative you select does not change the meaning of the sentence. If the meaning changes, select a different word or use a different sentence construction."
- Note: each word in the dictionary has its part of speech in parentheses, abbreviated as specified in the introduction to the dictionary.
- Note: if a word that you want to use is not in the dictionary: find that word in an English dictionary; find which is the best synonym that is approved in the STE dictionary; use the approved STE word or find a different sentence construction with other approved words.
- Examples (document):
  - "Test" is an approved noun, but not an approved verb.
    - STE: Test B is an alternative to test A.
    - Non-STE: Test the system for leaks.
    - STE: Do the leak test of the system. / or / STE: Do a test for leaks in the system.
  - "Dim" is an approved adjective, but not an approved verb.
    - STE: A dim light comes on.
    - Non-STE: Dim the lights.
    - STE: Set the lights to the dim position.
  - "Clean" (verb and adjective, both approved):
    - STE: Clean the inner surface of the container. ("Clean" is a verb here.)
    - STE: Make sure that the area is clean. ("Clean" is an adjective here.)
  - "Acceptable" (adjective, not approved; three approved alternatives of the same part of speech, word-for-word replacement):
    - Non-STE: A value of 2 mm is acceptable.
    - STE: A value of 2 mm is permitted.
  - "Operable" (adjective, not approved; approved alternative "operate" is a verb, so a different construction is needed):
    - Non-STE: Make sure that the valve is operable.
    - STE: Make sure that the valve can operate.
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) Do not turn a noun into a verb or the reverse ("Test the system" becomes "Do the test of the system"). The same applies to words such as "commit", "log", "build", "config", "ship".

### Rule 1.3 Use approved words only with their approved meanings.

- Each approved word in the dictionary has a specified approved meaning. Some of these words can have more restricted meanings compared with their meanings in standard English. Always use the approved words only with their approved meanings.
- The approved meaning of the verb "follow" is "come after, go after." You cannot use "follow" with other meanings that are not approved. For "to do that which the procedures or instructions tell you", use "obey".
- Examples (document):
  - STE: Do the procedures that follow:
  - STE: Follow the green lights to the nearest staircase.
  - Non-STE: Follow the safety instructions.
  - STE: Obey the safety instructions.
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) Check each common verb for its restricted meaning, for example "follow", "run", "handle", "support", "fail". If the sentence uses another meaning, pick the approved word for that meaning.

### Rule 1.4 Use only the approved forms of verbs and adjectives.

- The dictionary gives each approved verb together with its approved forms. It also gives the approved adjectives in their base form, with their comparative and superlative forms in parentheses where applicable.
- Verb example: REMOVE (v), REMOVES, REMOVED, REMOVED. You can use "remove" as follows:

| Infinitive/Imperative forms | Simple present tense | Simple past tense | Past participle form (as an adjective) |
|---|---|---|---|
| (To) Remove/Remove | Remove(s) | Removed | Removed |

- "The past participle form of the verb is usually the same as the simple past tense. Thus, the dictionary gives it two times."
- Adjective example: SLOW (adj) (SLOWER, SLOWEST). You can use "slow" as follows:

| Base form | Comparative form | Superlative form |
|---|---|---|
| Slow | Slower | Slowest |

- Adjectives that make their comparative and superlative forms with "more" and "most" do not have these forms in the dictionary. This is because "more" and "most" are approved words.
- Examples: the document gives the two tables above and no Non-STE/STE pairs.
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) Use only the verb forms and tenses of rule 3.2: infinitive, imperative, simple present, simple past, simple future ("will" + base form) and the past participle as an adjective. No continuous or perfect forms. Section 3 has the detail.

### Rule 1.5 You can use words that you can include in a technical noun category.

- "A technical noun is a noun term that refers to a specified concept and is applicable to a subject field."
- The dictionary does not include technical nouns because there are too many, and each subject field uses different technical nouns for their texts.
- You can find many of these technical nouns in your company glossary or terminology database. STE gives a list of categories, with examples, to help you: select technical nouns to put in your company glossary or terminology database; use technical nouns correctly.
- You can use technical nouns in procedural and descriptive writing if you can include them in one or more of these twenty-two categories.
- Note: the technical nouns in their related categories are only examples. Rule 1.5 does not give a full list of all possible technical nouns. The listed words in each category have uppercase letters only when it is necessary. For example, official identifications, titles, and abbreviations.
- Note on colors (category 17): colors are adjectives, but STE identifies them as technical nouns. Comparative and superlative forms of colors (for example, blacker, the reddest) are not permitted in STE.

### Rule 1.6 Use a word that is not approved in the dictionary, only when it is a technical noun or part of a technical noun.

- The dictionary includes some words that you cannot use because they are not approved. But if you can put these words in an applicable category of technical nouns, you can use them as technical nouns in some contexts.
- The same word can go into different categories of technical nouns, when you use it with different meanings in different contexts.
- "If a word is not in the dictionary, you can use it if it is part of a technical noun." For example, "relative" is not in the dictionary, but it is part of the technical noun "relative angular position".
- Examples (document):
  - "Base" is not approved in the dictionary; its alternative is "bottom (n)."
    - Non-STE: Make sure that the two spigots at the base of the unit engage. ("Base" is not permitted when it is related to a surface.)
    - STE: Make sure that the two spigots at the bottom of the unit engage.
    - STE: The base of the triangle is 5 cm. ("Base" is a technical noun, category 7.)
    - STE: Access to the base is permitted between 9 a.m. and 6 p.m. ("Base" is a technical noun, category 5.)
  - "Backup" is not approved; its alternatives are "emergency (n)" and "auxiliary (adj)." It is a one-word technical noun, and "backup file" is a two-word technical noun.
    - STE: Do the backup of the computer at regular intervals. (Category 19.)
    - STE: Keep the backup file in a safe area. (Category 19.)
    - Non-STE: For this procedure, make sure that one person is available as backup. ("Backup" is not permitted and you cannot use it in this context.)
    - STE: Two persons are necessary to do this procedure. (Different sentence construction.)
  - "Main" is not approved; its alternative is "primary (adj)."
    - Non-STE: The laptop has these main parts: ("Main part" is not a technical noun, and it is correct to replace "main" with "primary.")
    - STE: The laptop has these primary parts:
    - STE: Retract the main landing gear. ("Main landing gear" is a technical noun. Replacing "main" with "primary" is incorrect here, because "primary landing gear" is not the technical noun approved in your company, industry, or subject field.)
  - "Relative" is not in the dictionary; "angular" and "position" are approved.
    - STE: This procedure gives instructions to identify the relative angular positions of the power-turbine rotor disks. ("Relative" is part of a technical noun, category 7.)
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) A banned word can stay when it is a glossary term used as a noun with that meaning ("backup", "base branch"). Use the approved alternative when the word is used in its general meaning.

### Rule 1.7 Do not use words that are technical nouns as verbs.

- Use a technical noun only as a noun or as an adjective that is part of a different technical noun. Do not use the same word as a verb. Use a different sentence construction that lets you use the word as a technical noun.
- Some words can be technical nouns and technical verbs in some contexts, when you can put the word in a technical noun category (rule 1.5) and in a technical verb category (rule 1.12). Then the noun use and the verb use are each correct in their own sentence.
- Examples (document):
  - "Oil" is a technical noun, category 4.
    - Non-STE: Oil the steel surfaces.
    - STE: Apply oil to the steel surfaces.
  - "Snow" is a technical noun, category 16.
    - Non-STE: If you think it will snow, make sure that the vehicle is in the applicable configuration.
    - STE: If you think that snow will fall, make sure that the vehicle is in the applicable configuration.
  - "Drill" as noun and as verb (both in STE):
    - STE: If the surface is hard, use a carbide drill. ("Drill" is a technical noun, category 3.)
    - STE: Drill a hole at the intersection of the two lines. ("Drill" is a technical verb, rule 1.12, category 1 a), manufacturing processes, remove material.)
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) Do not use a glossary noun as a verb ("Log the error" becomes "Write the error to the log"; "Branch the repo" becomes "Create a branch of the repo"). A word that is in both lists, such as "store" (category 19 noun, category 2 b) verb), is allowed in each use.

### Rule 1.8 Use technical nouns that are approved in your company, industry, or subject field.

- If your company, industry, or subject field has an approved technical noun for a system, component, part, or process, use that technical noun. Usually, such technical nouns are included in official parts information and in company documentation.
- Example (document):
  - STE: The front panel of the phone has a touchscreen and a home button. ("Touchscreen" and "home button" are technical nouns that are approved in your company, industry, or subject field.)
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) Use the name the codebase, the glossary (GLOSSARY.md) and the tools use for a thing, for example the existing identifier for a service or a config key.

### Rule 1.9 When you must select a technical noun, use one which is short and easy to understand.

- When there is no technical noun that is approved in your company, industry, or subject field, select one that is short (not more than three words) and easy to understand.
- In the example, it is sufficient to use the words "screws," "flange," and "cover" because these parts have index numbers and the related illustration clearly identifies them. "Differently, add one or two adjectives to the noun to help your reader understand."
- Example (document):
  - Non-STE: Remove the four stainless steel pan head machine screws (10) that attach the metallic machined flange (15) to the front housing cover (20).
  - STE: Remove the four screws (10) that attach the flange (15) to the cover (20).
  - (It is sufficient to use "screws," "flange," and "cover" because the parts have index numbers and the illustration identifies them.)
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) A name for a new thing has at most three words. Add one or two adjectives only when the reader needs them to tell two things apart.

### Rule 1.10 Do not use regional, slang, or jargon words as technical nouns.

- Technical words that only persons in confined regions or geographical areas use are not easy to understand for persons from a different region or area. When you select technical nouns, always use well-known words.
- The rule is also applicable to technical slang or jargon words. "If only a small number of persons understand a word, it will cause confusion and non-effective communication."
- Examples (document):
  - Non-STE: During logging operations, attach a choker to the heavy machinery to hold the logs in their position. (A "choker" is a type of cable used in logging operations to attach logs during transportation. It is a term used in some regions of North America and Canada, Northern Europe, and New Zealand. Its meaning is not immediately clear to the reader.)
  - STE: During logging operations, attach a cable to the heavy machinery to hold the logs in their position.
  - Non-STE: After the firmware update, do not brick the router. ("Brick" is technical slang in information technology, with the meaning of "to make a device non-operational." Its meaning is not immediately clear to the reader.)
  - STE: After the firmware update, do not set the router to OFF.
  - Non-STE: Remove your gear from the work area. ("Gear" is technical jargon that refers to tools and equipment, and its meaning is not immediately clear to the reader.)
  - STE: Remove your tools and equipment from the work area.
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) Avoid team slang and idioms in commits, MRs and comments ("nuke", "bump", "yak shave", "flaky", "footgun", "green"). Write the plain term.

### Rule 1.11 Do not use different technical nouns for the same item.

- When you select a technical noun, do not use a different technical noun in other parts of your text to refer to the same item. Use the technical noun that is approved in your company, industry, or subject field, and then always use it in your text.
- Example (document):
  - Non-STE: 1. Make sure that the servo control unit is in the open position. 2. Do the operational test of the actuator. 3. Disconnect the control unit from the test rig.
  - STE: 1. Make sure that the actuator is in the open position. 2. Do the operational test of the actuator. 3. Disconnect the actuator from the test rig.
  - ("Servo control unit," "actuator," and "control unit" refer to the same item. If the approved technical noun is "actuator," always use it.)
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) Pick one name per thing in a commit, MR or comment. Do not alternate "service", "app" and "component" for one program.

### Rule 1.12 You can use verbs that you can include in a technical verb category.

- A technical verb is a verb term that refers to a specified concept or process and is applicable to a subject field. The dictionary does not include technical verbs because there are too many, and each subject field uses different technical verbs.
- You can find many of these technical verbs in your company glossary or terminology database. STE gives a list of categories, with examples, to help you select technical verbs to put in your glossary and to use technical verbs correctly.
- "Technical verbs must obey the same rules as other approved verbs in STE. Refer to section 3."
- You can use technical verbs in procedural and descriptive texts if you can include them in one or more of these four categories.
- Note: the technical verbs in their related categories are only examples. Rule 1.12 does not give a full list of all possible technical verbs.

The four categories (verbatim, with the document's example words):

1. Manufacturing processes
   Terms that give instructions and information to:
   a) Remove material: drill, grind, mill, ream, unsolder
   b) Add material: flame, insulate, remetal, retread
   c) Attach material: braze, crimp, solder, weld
   d) Change the mechanical strength, the structure, or the physical properties of a material: anneal, cure, decay, freeze, heat-treat, magnetize, normalize, vaporize
   e) Change the surface finish of a material: buff, burnish, dress, passivate, plate, polish
   f) Change the shape of a material: blend, cast, extrude, spin, stamp
2. Computer processes and applications
   Terms that give instructions and information for:
   a) Input and output processes: click, digitize, enter, press, print, swipe, tap, type
   b) User interface and application processes: clear, close, copy, cut, delete, deselect, disable, drag, drag and drop, enable, encrypt, erase, filter, highlight, invalidate, maximize, minimize, navigate, open, paste, save, scroll, sort, store, tweet, validate, zoom in, zoom out
   c) System operations: abort, boot, communicate, debug, download, format, install, load, manage, process, reboot, update, upgrade, upload
3. Instructions and information for applicable subject fields
   Terms that give instructions and information in these contexts:
   a) Engineering, mathematical, and scientific: bisect, compensate for, convert, detect, float, modulate, radiate, transform, sink
   b) Medical: disinfect, intubate, operate, prescribe, sanitize, sterilize
   c) Civil and military operations: aim, arm, detect, disable, dry-motor, enable, explode, fire, inhibit, intercept, lase, load, lock on, unlatch, unload, wet-motor, parachute
   d) Navigation: approach, descend, deviate, fly, hover, land, maintain, navigate, retrim, take off, trim, respond, taxi
   e) Automotive and railway: accelerate, brake, couple, crank, crash, decouple, dispatch, drift, inflate, park, qualify, steer
   f) Energy, oil, and gas: compress, distill, drill, emit, extract, inject, pump
4. Law and regulations
   Terms that give instructions and information only for legal and regulatory texts. For example, contracts, warranty texts, certificates, standards and specifications, and legal papers.
   acknowledge, comply with, communicate, conform to, describe, enforce, explain, meet (a requirement), inform, modify, notify, omit, regulate, sign, supersede, understand, waive (for inspection and requirements)

Conditions on using a technical verb (from the explanation):
- If an approved verb in the dictionary accurately gives the instruction or the information, use the approved verb. "Do not use a technical verb if it is possible to write the same sentence with the words (verbs and other related words) that are approved in the dictionary."
- If you must use a technical verb, use only one that is correct in your context. Do not use technical verbs that are general or not clear.
- Do not use a technical verb if it is not necessary. If possible, use a verb that is approved in the dictionary and an applicable technical noun.
- Some words are not approved in the dictionary but can be technical verbs if you can put them in the specified categories. In a different context they are not permitted.

Examples (document):
- Not-necessary or wrongly chosen technical verb:
  - Non-STE: If you detect broken wires, repair them. ("Detect" is not approved and cannot be a technical verb in this context.)
  - STE: If you find broken wires, repair them.
  - STE: The security scanner detects metal objects. ("Detect" is the correct technical verb in this context.)
- General or unclear technical verb:
  - Non-STE: Machine the hole until it has a diameter of 8.00 +/- 0.003 mm.
  - STE: Ream the hole until it has a diameter of 8.00 +/- 0.003 mm.
- Use a dictionary verb and a technical noun instead of the technical verb:
  - Non-STE: Clamp the cable in position. ("Clamp" is a technical noun, category 1.)
  - STE: Put clamps on the cable to hold it in position.
  - Non-STE: Grease the fasteners. ("Grease" is a technical noun, category 4.)
  - STE: Apply grease to the fasteners.
  - Non-STE: Wire the cable to the structure. ("Wire" is a technical noun, category 1.)
  - STE: Attach the cable to the structure with wire.
- Not-approved word that is a technical verb only in its category:
  - STE: Enter your password. ("Enter" is a technical verb, category 2 a), computer processes and applications, input and output processes.)
  - Non-STE: Do not enter the engine test area without approval. ("Enter" is not permitted when it is in a different context.)
  - STE: Do not go into the engine test area without approval.
  - STE: If the tower does not respond, use a different channel. ("Respond" is a technical verb, category 3 d), navigation.)
  - Non-STE: If the instrument fails to respond, do a test. ("Respond" is not permitted when it is in a different context.)
  - STE: If the instrument does not operate correctly, do a test.
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) Category 2 covers most software verbs: click, enter, press, type, copy, delete, save, open, close, install, update, download, upload, boot, debug. Use them for those actions. Use "merge", "rebase", "deploy" or "refactor" only if they are in the project glossary as technical verbs and no approved verb says the same. The verbs follow the verb-form rules of section 3.

### Rule 1.13 Do not use technical verbs as nouns.

- In English, words that look the same do not always have the same function in a sentence. Use technical verbs only as verbs, not as nouns.
- You can use the past participle form of technical verbs as adjectives.
- Some words can be technical verbs and technical nouns in some contexts, when you can put the word in a technical verb category (rule 1.12) and in a technical noun category (rule 1.5).
- Examples (document):
  - Non-STE: Give the hole 0.20-inch ream.
  - STE: Ream the hole to a 0.20-inch dimension.
  - STE: Lubricate the reamed hole. (The adjective "reamed" is the past participle form of the technical verb "ream." "Reamed hole" is a technical noun.)
  - STE: Make sure that the plate is not damaged. ("Plate" is a technical noun, category 1.)
  - STE: There are two methods to plate the ring nut (2). ("Plate" is a technical verb, category 1 c), manufacturing processes, attach material.)
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) Do not use a verb-only term as a noun ("a download", "an install", "a rebase" for the action). Write "Download the file" or use a noun term that is in the glossary. The past participle as an adjective is allowed ("the deleted branch", "the updated file").

### Rule 1.14 Use American English spelling unless other official directives tell you differently.

- Use the spelling specified in the STE dictionary (American English spelling). Use a different spelling only if other technical publication specifications, style guides, contracts, or other official directives are applicable.
- Note: if there is quoted text that has British English spelling, for example on a computer screen, do not change the spelling. Keep the quoted text as it is. Refer to Rule 8.6, which tells you how to use quoted texts correctly.
- Examples (document):
  - Non-STE: The door is made of carbon fibre reinforced plastic. ("Fibre" is British English spelling.)
  - STE: The door is made of carbon-fiber-reinforced plastic. ("Fiber" is American English spelling.)
  - Non-STE: Change the colour of the display. ("Colour" is British English spelling.)
  - STE: Change the color of the display. ("Color" is American English spelling.)
- Application to software prose: (interpretation) Use "color", "behavior", "initialize", "center" in prose. Do not change identifiers, UI strings or quoted error messages that have another spelling; keep them as they are.

## Section 2 - Multi-word nouns

Source: ASD-STE100 Issue 9 (2025-01-15), Part 1 - Writing rules, pages 1-2-1 to 1-2-4.

### Summary of the rules (verbatim)

Multi-word nouns

| Rule | Text |
|---|---|
| Rule 2.1 | Write multi-word nouns of no more than three words. |
| Rule 2.2 | When a technical noun has more than three words, write it in full. Then, you can use one of these methods to make the technical noun clear: - Give a shorter form of the technical noun. - Use hyphens (-) between words that you use as one unit. |

### Rule 2.1 Write multi-word nouns of no more than three words.

- In English, you can use one or more words to describe or modify a noun. Technical texts can contain long groups of words that have the function of one part of speech in a sentence. Usually, these groups of words are made of nouns and/or adjectives and are the subject or the object in a sentence. Such groups of words are known as multi-word nouns.
- Document's general examples: "Horizontal cylinder pivot bearing" (4 words), "Stainless steel corrosion protection strips" (5 words), "Actuator operating rod" (3 words).
- Why: the words in a long multi-word noun can connect to each other in different ways. The main noun (head noun) is usually the last word. When the connections are not clear, ambiguity occurs. Short multi-word nouns are easier to understand.
- "Runway light connection" is a short multi-word noun (3 words), the main noun is "connection". "Runway light connection resistance calibration" is long (5 words), the main noun is "calibration". It does not tell the reader the relation between "runway" and "calibration". The reader must understand four modifiers before the main noun.
- Non-native readers have more problems because in some languages the main noun comes first. More words make the noun less clear.
- Keep multi-word nouns to a maximum of three words.
- To keep them short, use prepositions (for example "of", "on", "in", "for") and explain the multi-word nouns.
- Examples (verbatim):
  - Non-STE: Runway light connection resistance calibration. (5 words)
    STE: Calibration of the resistance of the runway light connection. (1 word, 1 word, and 3 words)
  - Non-STE: Install the forward turbine overheat thermocouple terminal tags. (6 words)
    STE: Install the terminal tags on the forward overheat thermocouple of the turbine. (2 words and 3 words)
  - Non-STE: Remove the engine transmission housing attachment bolts. (5 words)
    STE: Remove the bolts that attach the transmission housing to the engine. (1 word, 1 word, 2 words, and 1 word)
  - Non-STE: Adjust to obtain door operating rod alignment with the attachment point. (4 words)
    STE: Adjust the door operating rod until it aligns with the attachment point. (3 words and 1 word)
- Application to software prose (interpretation, not document text): Count every word in the group, adjectives and identifiers included. Split "retry queue error handler config" into "the config of the error handler for the retry queue". A code identifier written as one token (for example `retryQueueHandler`) is one word, but do not use that to hide a long chain of words.

### Rule 2.2 When a technical noun has more than three words, write it in full. Then, you can use one of these methods to make the technical noun clear: - Give a shorter form of the technical noun. - Use hyphens (-) between words that you use as one unit.

- A long multi-word noun can be a long technical noun, or a combination of shorter technical nouns. Often you cannot divide technical nouns into smaller parts, because they are the technical nouns that your company, industry or subject field uses. Write them as they are, in their approved form.
- Method 1 - Shorter form of technical nouns:
  - If a long technical noun comes from an official document (for example an engineering drawing or an illustrated parts catalog), write it in full the first time it occurs in the text.
  - Then, if possible, explain the technical noun. In the remaining text use a shorter form or an approved abbreviation.
  - The shorter form must obey rule 2.1 (in the example, "safety connector pin" has three words).
  - The explanation is not necessary when the text gives all the necessary information about the unit. You write all official technical nouns that include more than three nouns in full the first time they occur. In the remaining text you use their related approved abbreviations.
  - If an approved technical noun includes three words or less, it is not necessary to use abbreviations.
  - You can use abbreviations from your official company documentation, but be careful. A text full of abbreviations in a procedure, although shorter, is not easy to read.
  - Example in STE (verbatim): "Before you do this procedure, engage the ramp service door safety connector pin (the pin that holds the ramp service door, referred to in this procedure as the "safety connector pin")."
    In this example, you write "ramp service door safety connector pin" in full. Then, after an explanation, you give a shorter technical noun: "safety connector pin." This shorter technical noun has three words and obeys rule 2.1.
  - Example in STE (verbatim, abbreviations after full first use): "The Main Fuel Metering Unit (MFMU) is an aluminum alloy unit that includes a Main Engine Control Unit (MECU) and a Distribution Block (DB). The MFMU is installed in the engine bypass duct and operates in the engine fuel system. The function of the MFMU is to meter and supply the fuel from the Main Engine Fuel Pump (MEFP) to the fuel manifolds and the starter jets. The Digital Engine Control Unit (DECU) sends electrical signals to operate the MFMU."
  - Do not write: "The primary parts of the valve are: - The DA (8) - The PVA (15) - The BA (17) - The VB (20)."
    WRITE: "The primary parts of the valve are: - The diaphragm assembly (8) - The poppet valve assembly (15) - The bush assembly (17) - The valve body (20)."
  - Do not write: "A. Remove the DA (8) from the VB (20). B. Remove the PVA (15) from its seat. C. Remove the BA (17) from the VB (20)."
    WRITE: "A. Remove the diaphragm assembly (8) from the valve body (20). B. Remove the poppet valve assembly (15) from its seat. C. Remove the bush assembly (17) from the valve body (20)."
- Method 2 - Hyphens (-) between the words that you use as one unit:
  - A hyphen connects words or parts of words. Use hyphens to show how related words operate as one unit. This makes the multi-word noun agree with rule 2.1. Hyphenated words always count as one word.
  - Examples in STE: "Make sure that the cutoff-switch power connection is safe." (3 words). "Inspection of the lavatory rapid-decompression device." (3 words).
  - Do not connect words that are not related: the hyphen changes the meaning. If you are not sure, only explain the multi-word noun. Then use a shorter form, or an official approved abbreviation.
  - If an approved technical noun includes hyphens, do not change it. If it is too long, write it in full the first time it occurs and then use the recommended method (shorter technical nouns) specified in this rule (Method 1).
  - Do not use hyphens to make groups of more than three words. If you use hyphens for all the words, this multi-word noun will not be easy to read and understand.
    Non-STE: Move the main-gear-door-retraction-winch handle. (2 words, but not correct)
    STE: Move the main-gear-door retraction-winch handle. (3 words)
  - If an approved technical noun has three words or less (for example "poppet valve assembly", "diaphragm assembly"), hyphens are not necessary.
    Do not write: "A. Remove the diaphragm-assembly (8) from the valve body (20). B. Remove the poppet-valve assembly (15) from its seat."
    WRITE: "A. Remove the diaphragm assembly (8) from the valve body (20). B. Remove the poppet valve assembly (15) from its seat."
  - If an approved technical noun includes a hyphen (for example "inward-outward valve"), do not remove the hyphen. Keep the noun from your official documentation.
    Do not write: "The inward outward valve is part of the fuel system."
    WRITE: "The inward-outward valve is part of the fuel system."
- Application to software prose (interpretation, not document text): A long official name (a service, a Helm chart, a config key) is written in full once, then a shorter form of at most three words, defined at first use. Do not hyphenate to dodge the limit, and do not replace a short name with an acronym. Keep a name that has a hyphen as the project writes it.

### Rule 3.1 Use only the verb forms that are given in the dictionary.

- The STE dictionary gives the verb forms you can use for each approved verb. Examples from the document: REMOVE (v) - REMOVES, REMOVED, REMOVED. GIVE (v) - GIVES, GAVE, GIVEN.
- The introduction to the dictionary in part 2 gives more information about the verb forms and how to use the approved verbs.
- Application to software prose (interpretation, not document text): Use the forms the dictionary lists for each verb. A word that is not an approved verb is not used as a verb (see rule 3.7).

### Rule 3.2 Use only these verb forms and tenses of verbs: - The infinitive form - The imperative form (command form) - The simple present tense - The simple past tense - The simple future tense - The past participle form (as an adjective).

- Use only the verb forms and tenses that are approved.
- Table in the document (verbatim content):
  - Infinitive form (base form): (To) Adjust (regular verb); (To) Give (irregular verb).
  - Imperative form (command form): Adjust + object; Give + object.
  - Simple present tense: You/we/they adjust, It adjusts; You/we/they give, It gives.
  - Simple past tense: You/we/they adjusted, It adjusted; You/we/they gave, It gave.
  - Simple future tense: You/we/they will adjust, It will adjust; You/we/they will give, It will give.
  - Past participle form (as an adjective): The adjusted linkage; The given information.
- Do not use other forms and tenses that are not approved, for example:
  - The present perfect (have/has adjusted)
  - The past perfect (had adjusted)
  - The present/past progressive (is/was adjusting)
  - And all other complex verb constructions.
- Application to software prose (interpretation, not document text): "I have fixed the test" becomes "I fixed the test". "The job is running" becomes "The job runs". Use "will" for the future ("This commit will change ...") or the simple present.

### Rule 3.3 Use the past participle form as an adjective.

- When used as an adjective, the past participle shows the condition of something. This is not passive voice.
- Use it:
  - Before a noun
  - After a verb form of the verbs "to be," "to become," or "to stay."
- Do not use the past participle form if it is not in the dictionary.
- Examples:
  - STE: Examine all parts of the disassembled unit for damage. ("Disassembled" is an adjective before the noun "unit". It shows the condition of the unit.)
  - STE: When the unit is fully disassembled, clean all the parts. ("Disassembled" is an adjective after the verb "to be" that shows the condition of the unit.)
- Some approved adjectives in the dictionary are the past participle form of verbs that are not approved, for example "permitted" and "damaged". Their approved part of speech is "(adj)", so you can use them.
  - STE: Do not put more than the permitted weight on the trolley.
  - STE: Make sure that the mating surfaces are not damaged.
- Application to software prose (interpretation, not document text): "The cached value", "the config is unchanged", "the file stays locked" are conditions and are allowed when the word is in the dictionary. "The file is deleted by the job" names an agent, so it is passive (rule 3.6).

### Rule 3.4 Do not use auxiliary verbs to make complex verb constructions.

- Do not use the past participle as a verb form together with the auxiliary "have". This makes a tense that is not approved.
  - Non-STE: The operator has adjusted the linkage. (The present perfect tense is not approved.)
  - STE: The operator adjusted the linkage. (The simple past tense is approved.)
- Some complex verb constructions include other auxiliary verbs with the past participle as a verb. Sentences with these become complex sentences in the passive voice.
  - Non-STE: The seat is to be installed before you install the cushion
    STE: Before you install the cushion, install the seat.
  - Non-STE: The volume control can be adjusted.
    STE: You can adjust the volume control.
  - Non-STE: The temperature must be adjusted.
    STE: Adjust the temperature.
  - Non-STE: The sleeve will be adjusted by the robot.
    STE: The robot will adjust the sleeve.
- Application to software prose (interpretation, not document text): the STE examples above keep a modal verb followed by a base verb ("You can adjust the volume control"). What rule 3.4 bans is an auxiliary plus a past participle used as a verb.
- Application to software prose (interpretation, not document text): Rewrite "The value must be set" as "Set the value" (instruction) or "The caller must set the value" (description). Rewrite "has been changed" as "changed".

### Rule 3.5 Use the "-ing" form of a verb only as a technical noun or as a modifier in a technical noun.

- Words with an "-ing" form can have different functions (different parts of speech). Examples: part of a verb to describe an action in the present ("Be careful while the door is opening."), adjective ("An opening door can be dangerous."), noun ("Opening a door can be dangerous."), and long groups of modifiers, noun phrases and dependent clauses ("A mechanic opening a door without obeying the specified safety precautions can easily cause injury to persons standing near the door."). These functions cause ambiguity or long, complex sentences. Thus words with an "-ing" form are usually not permitted.
- Examples:
  - Non-STE: When you are doing this procedure, obey all the safety precautions.
    STE: When you do this procedure, obey all the safety precautions.
  - Non-STE: Mechanics wearing insufficient protective clothing and opening containers containing hazardous materials in areas where there is a lack of ventilation, using inappropriate tools without observing the manufacturer's instructions, are in danger of coming into contact with these materials and thus suffering from skin irritation and breathing problems.
    STE: Before you use dangerous materials, obey these precautions: (1) Read the manufacturer's instructions. (2) Make sure that there is sufficient airflow in the work area. (3) Put on a face mask and protective clothing. (4) Get the correct tools to open the containers for these materials. If you do not obey these precautions, injury to your skin and your lungs can occur.
- "-ing" words that are technical nouns or parts of technical nouns:
  - As a technical noun (for example in procedural titles or headings): Cleaning, Testing and Fault Isolation, Handling, Packaging, Shipping, Troubleshooting.
  - As a modifier in a technical noun: an adjective related to the function of a system, component, part, tool, material or equipment. Examples: Air-conditioning system, degreasing agent, grinding wheel, polishing disc, sanding machine, switching relay, welding torch.
- Approved words that have an "-ing" form (only a small number in the dictionary):
  - Nouns: lighting, opening, routing, servicing
  - Adjectives: mating, missing, remaining
  - A pronoun: something
  - A preposition: during
- Application to software prose (interpretation, not document text): Avoid "-ing" in sentences ("When you run the job", not "When running the job"). A heading such as "Troubleshooting" and a name such as "logging config" or "testing framework" are technical nouns. Commit subjects in imperative form have no "-ing" problem.

### Rule 3.6 Use the active voice. In descriptive writing, you can use the passive voice only if the agent is unknown.

- Technical texts consist of procedural writing and descriptive writing. Always use the active voice in STE. In descriptive writing, the passive voice is permitted only when the agent (the person or thing that does the action) is unknown.
- Active voice: the subject does the action ("A" does "B"); the grammatical subject is the logical subject (agent). Passive voice: the subject receives the action ("B" is done by "A"); the grammatical subject is B and the agent is A.
  - Active: The manufacturer gives the safety procedures. / Passive: The safety procedures are given by the manufacturer.
  - Active: The side stay holds the main gear leg. / Passive: The main gear leg is held by the side stay.
- Test for passive: think of the question "by whom or by what?" If the text answers it, it is passive. The preposition "by" is a good indication. The object of "by" is the agent, and you can use it as the subject of an active sentence. A passive construction does not always contain an agent: "The dimensions are given in the table." "The main gear leg is held in its position."
- A sentence in the active voice always has a grammatical subject (the agent). In the passive sentence in the example below, the agent is unknown (and we do not know the cause of data corruption). In the active sentence, the agent ("transmission") is incorrect ("transmission" is not the cause of data corruption), and the meaning of the sentence is different. Thus, the active sentence becomes technically incorrect:
  - Passive: During transmission, the data was corrupted. (Correct, the agent is unknown.)
  - Active: During transmission, something corrupted the data. (Correct, you do not know the identity of "something", but you can use it as the agent.)
  - Active: Transmission corrupted the data. (Incorrect, "transmission" is not the correct agent.)
  - In the example, if you use the word "something" ("a thing that is not determined or specified") as the agent, the active voice is technically correct.
- Four methods to change passive to active:
  - Method 1: When the sentence gives the agent (usually the object of "by"), put the agent at the start and use it as the subject. The subject must always be the noun that does the action. (The diagram that the standard shows here is not in the page text.)
    Non-STE: The circuits are connected by a switching relay. (Passive)
    STE: A switching relay connects the circuits. (Active)
  - Method 2: Change an infinitive verb to an active verb.
    Non-STE: These values are used by the computer to calculate the energy consumption. (Passive)
    STE: The computer calculates the energy consumption from these values. (Active)
    ("are used by" gives no important information here, so use the verb "calculate".)
  - Method 3: In procedural writing, change the verb to the imperative (command) form.
    Non-STE: The test can be continued by the operator. (Passive)
    STE: Continue the test. (Active)
    Non-STE: Oil and grease are to be removed with a degreasing agent. (Passive)
    STE: Remove oil and grease with a degreasing agent. (Active)
  - Method 4: When the agent is not given, use "you" or "we" as subjects in the active form. If the agent is the reader, use "you". If the agent is your company or organization, use "we".
    Non-STE: On the ground, the valve can be opened with the override handle. (Passive)
    STE: On the ground, you can open the valve with the override handle. (Active)
    Non-STE: Additives are not used in this type of fuel. (Passive)
    STE: We do not use additives in this type of fuel. (Active) or STE: This type of fuel does not contain additives.
- For complex passive sentences with auxiliary verbs, decide whether you write a procedural or a descriptive sentence:
  - Non-STE: The volume control can be adjusted. / STE: Adjust the volume control. (Procedural sentence) / STE: You can adjust the volume control. (Descriptive sentence)
  - Non-STE: The oil temperature must be adjusted before the start of the test. / STE: Before you start the test, adjust the oil temperature. (Procedural sentence) / STE: Before the start of the test, the operator must adjust the oil temperature. (Descriptive sentence)
  - Non-STE: The valve will be adjusted during the test. / STE: During the test, adjust the valve. (Procedural sentence) / STE: NOTE: You will adjust the valve during the test. (Descriptive sentence)
  - Non-STE: The component is to be installed before you do the test. / STE: Before you do the test, install the component. (Procedural sentence) / STE: Before the test, the operator must install the component. (Descriptive sentence)
- Application to software prose (interpretation, not document text): "The build is triggered by the push" becomes "The push triggers the build". "The data was corrupted" is allowed in a description only when the cause is unknown; or "something corrupted the data". Use "you" for the reader and "we" for the team.

### Rule 3.7 Use an approved verb to describe an action, not a noun or other parts of speech.

- There can be different ways to give the same information in STE. If an approved verb describes the action, use it. Verbs describe actions more clearly than nouns or other parts of speech. The "do not write" sentences are still STE, but the direct verbs are clearer.
  - Do not write: The ohmmeter gives an indication of 450 ohms. / WRITE: The ohmmeter shows 450 ohms.
  - Do not write: Before the removal of the unit, make sure that the power supply is OFF. / WRITE: Before you remove the unit, make sure that the power supply is OFF.
- If a word is not approved as a verb in the dictionary, do not use it as a verb. Use a different sentence construction to give the same information.
  - Non-STE: Check the laptop battery. / STE: Do a check of the laptop battery.
- Application to software prose (interpretation, not document text): "Perform a validation of the input" becomes "Validate the input" when "validate" is an approved verb. If the word is not approved as a verb, use "do a check of" style: the verb is a plain approved one and the word is the noun.

- Rule 4.2: Do not omit words or use contractions to make your sentences shorter.

(Note: the summary table says "complex texts"; the rule box in the body says "complex text".)

### Section intro (general principle)

- "The basic principle of STE is to make texts easy to read and understand. Thus, you must write short sentences and use clear sentence structures."
- STE has different rules for sentences in procedures (section 5) and in descriptive text (section 6). Section 4 holds the general rules that apply to both types of writing.

### Rule 4.1 Write short and clear sentences.

- Write short and clear sentences that give accurate instructions and information.
- In procedures, give short and clear instructions directly to the reader (imperative form).
- In a descriptive text, make sure that each sentence has only one topic (subject or idea) and does not contain the imperative form. Then, in the sentences that follow, gradually give information about that topic.
- For both types of writing, always make sure the text is not abstract. It must clearly show how to do a task or how a system operates. Be accurate. Do not give information that is not accurate or can have different meanings.
- To make your writing more accurate, give the specified temperatures and the related cure times.

Examples (procedure):
- Non-STE: To remove the cover assembly (9), first remove the four screws (10) that attach the cover (11) to the housing (12), and then, after taking the cover (11) off the housing (12), remove the preformed packing (13) and throw it away.
- STE: 1. Remove the cover assembly (9) as follows: A. Remove the four screws (10) that attach the cover (11) to the housing (12). B. Remove the cover (11) from the housing (12). C. Remove and discard the preformed packing (13).

Example (descriptive, one topic per sentence):
- Non-STE: The side stay assembly has two folding toggles hinged together and attached with hinges between the main gear strut and the side stay bracket. (More than one topic. Write a new sentence for each topic.)
- STE: The side stay assembly has two folding toggles. The folding toggles are attached together with hinges. These folding toggles are also attached with hinges between the main gear strut and the side stay bracket. (Three sentences, each with its own topic.)

Examples (abstract text):
- Do not write: No leaks are permitted. (Does not tell the reader about the action.) / WRITE: Make sure that there are no leaks.
- Do not write: Different temperatures will change the cure time. (Abstract: no information about how the cure time changes.) / WRITE: When the temperature increases, the cure time will decrease. / WRITE: The cure time is 2 hours at a temperature of 20 °C.

Application to software prose (interpretation): Each sentence in a reply, commit body or MR description makes one point. State the concrete fact, value or action ("The cache TTL is 60 s") and not an abstract claim ("Performance may vary"). In a procedure, write the command, not a description of it.

### Rule 4.2 Do not omit words or use contractions to make your sentences shorter.

- Each sentence must have all its parts. Do not omit words or use contractions (for example "don't," "isn't," "aren't"). The sentence is shorter but not easier to read. Write all the words in full.
- Do not omit nouns: the reader cannot understand the meaning.
- Do not omit verbs: the reader will not understand the action.
- Do not omit the subject: the reader will not understand the action.
- Do not omit articles: omitted articles can cause ambiguity.
- Do not omit parts of words to make contractions: contractions are not easy to understand.

Examples:
- Non-STE: Can be a maximum of five inches long. / STE: Cracks can have a maximum length of five inches.
- Non-STE: Rotary switch to INPUT. / STE: Set the rotary switch to INPUT.
- Non-STE: If installed, remove the shims. / STE: If shims are installed, remove them.
- Non-STE: WARNING: MAKE SURE THAT THE POTABLE WATER SYSTEM IS NOT PRESSURIZED. IF NOT, THIS CAN CAUSE INJURY TO PERSONS. / STE: WARNING: MAKE SURE THAT THE POTABLE WATER SYSTEM IS NOT PRESSURIZED. A PRESSURIZED SYSTEM CAN CAUSE INJURY TO PERSONS.
- Non-STE: Remove the bolt and stop. / STE: Remove the bolt and the stop.
- Non-STE: If your hands are wet, don't touch the USB power adapter. / STE: If your hands are wet, do not touch the USB power adapter.

Application to software prose (interpretation): No telegraphic commit subjects or replies that drop the subject, verb or article ("Fix crash on null", "Updated config, tested"). No "don't", "can't", "it's". Name the subject: "The parser crashes on a null input."

### Rule 4.3 Use a vertical list for complex text.

- When the sentence is long and you must include many different items (for example a list of components, parts or documents) or actions, you can put them in a vertical list. Vertical lists make long and complex texts much easier to read and understand.
- When you make a vertical list:
  - Put a colon (:) at the end of the first sentence, before the first item.
  - Identify each item with a number, letter, punctuation mark or symbol. For example: a dash (-), a bullet point (•), a letter (a, b, c…), a number (1, 2, 3…).
  - Start each item with an uppercase letter.
  - Where applicable, use an article before the noun that is the subject of each item.
  - Put a period at the end of an item if it is a full sentence.
  - Do not put a period at the end of an item if it is not a full sentence.
  - Do not put a comma or a semicolon at the end of an item.
  - Put a period at the end of the last item.
  - For the marks or symbols to use, refer to the applicable specifications for technical publications, style guides and other official directives.
- Vertical lists can be used in procedural and in descriptive writing, but the two types of writing cannot be mixed in the same vertical list.
- In safety instructions, include negative commands (DO NOT) where necessary for each item in the list. This makes the safety instruction more direct and easier to understand.
- Each item must connect clearly and correctly to the first part of the list (the text before the colon).
- The layout must be easy to read. Do not nest a second vertical list in the primary list. Use the same level for all items.
- An item in a vertical list can contain a verb and not be a full sentence. Then, you do not use a period at the end of that item. (In the example, the last item still ends with a period, see the last bullet above.)

Examples (items are parts and documents, dashes or bullets):
- Non-STE: The wheel assembly comprises the tire, the tube, the spokes, the spoke fittings, the valve, and the hub. / STE: The wheel assembly has these parts: - The tire - The tube - The spokes - The spoke fittings - The valve - The hub.
- Non-STE: The report must include each of the following: a completed REC-1 form, a three-view drawing of the unit, a photograph of the unit, a copy of the source data. / STE: The report must include: • A completed REC-1 form • A three-view drawing of the unit • A photograph of the unit • A copy of the source data.

Examples (procedure and description, letters and numbers):
- Non-STE: If the RAT is retracted, remove the tag, open the isolating valves and, ensuring that there is no possibility of fouling, slowly extend the RAT to its full stroke. Close the isolating valves; tag "DO NOT OPERATE." Release the DOWN pressure. / STE: If the Ram Air Turbine (RAT) is retracted: (a) Remove the tag. (b) Open the isolating valves. (c) Slowly and fully extend the RAT and make sure that it does not touch other parts. (d) Close the isolating valves. (e) Tag the isolating valves with "DO NOT OPERATE." (f) Release the DOWN pressure.
- Non-STE: During the final movement of the landing gear retraction, the door operating bar located on the leg contacts and turns the latch, withdrawing the roller from the slot and the second roller entraps the door operating bar. / STE: When the landing gear retracts: (1) The door operating bar on the leg touches and turns the latch. (2) The roller moves out of the slot. (3) The second roller keeps the door operating bar in position.

Example (safety, negative command in each item):
- Non-STE: CAUTION: WHEN YOU GET ACCESS TO THE REAR FUSELAGE THROUGH THE ACCESS PANEL, DO NOT: - PUT YOUR FEET ON THE APU LINE. - USE THE APU LINE AS A HANDLE.
- STE: CAUTION: WHEN YOU GET ACCESS TO THE REAR FUSELAGE THROUGH THE ACCESS PANEL: - DO NOT PUT YOUR FEET ON THE APU LINE. - DO NOT USE THE APU LINE AS A HANDLE.

Example (item connects to the lead-in):
- Non-STE: Do not use acetone for cleaning these parts after the repair: - the service cabinet, - the toilet shrouds with the supports, - parts made of polycarbonate.
- STE: After the repair, do not use acetone to clean: - The service cabinet - The toilet shrouds - The toilet shroud supports - Parts made of polycarbonate.

Example (no nested list):
- Non-STE: Remove these parts: - The four screws (3) - The four washers (4) - The flange (2) that includes: (nested) - The two O-rings (6) - The seals (7) - The shims (8) - The spring (9).
- STE: Remove these parts: - The four screws (3) - The four washers (4) - The flange (2) (that includes the two O-rings (6) and the seals (7)) - The shims (8) - The spring (9).

Example (item with a verb, not a full sentence, no period):
- Non-STE: The middle part of the reservoir has the components that follow: - One check valve that prevents the backflow of fluid. - One air relief valve that opens at a pressure of 25 psi. - One transfer pipe connected to the pressurization manifold.
- STE: The middle part of the reservoir has the components that follow: - One check valve that prevents the backflow of fluid - One air relief valve that opens at a pressure of 25 psi - One transfer pipe connected to the pressurization manifold.

Application to software prose (interpretation): The document sets no minimum item count. It says "complex text", "many different items". Use a list for several parts, files or steps. Lead-in ends with a colon, items start with an uppercase letter, no nested lists in a short reply. Keep one list to all steps or all descriptions. Markdown bullets and numbered steps stand for the "dash/bullet/number" markers.

### Rule 4.4 Use connecting words and connecting phrases to connect sentences that contain related topics.

- Connecting words and phrases connect a topic in one sentence with an idea in the sentence that follows. In descriptive text they give a logical structure and information that is easy to understand.
- Some of the connecting words that are approved in the dictionary are "and," "but," "then," and "thus."
- "As a result" and "at the same time" are examples of connecting phrases you can use.
- You can also use demonstrative adjectives as connecting words to connect ideas in related sentences.
- In procedures, you can use these connecting words when an explanation is necessary after a work step. They can also be necessary in safety instructions to connect related sentences or make the text clear.

Examples in STE:
- The localizer course aligns with the centerline of the runway. And the glideslope path is at a constant angle to the threshold of the runway.
- These safety precautions are the minimum necessary for work in the pit lane. But the local regulations can give other necessary safety precautions.
- If the pressure increases, it changes the electrical currents in the transmitter. Thus, the power unit supplies currents to the indicator on the related panel.
- When the hydraulic pressure is released, the sequence valve moves to the open position. As a result, the actuators are connected to the hydraulic return.
- Tag the disassembled parts. This method will help you during the subsequent procedures.
- WARNING: ALWAYS USE PROTECTIVE EQUIPMENT IN THIS AREA. THIS PRECAUTION WILL PREVENT INJURY.

Application to software prose (interpretation): Link a cause and its effect in two short sentences with "Thus" or "As a result", not in one long sentence. A short reason after a step can start with "This".

### Rule 4.5 When applicable, use an article (the, a, an) or a demonstrative adjective (this, these) before a noun or a multi-word noun.

- Articles and demonstrative adjectives show the position of nouns and multi-word nouns in the sentence. Use them correctly. Do not omit them to make the text shorter.
- It is not always correct English to put an article before a noun. Do not use articles in general statements or concepts (no specified item; general concept; abstract quality).
- In short sentences it can be clearer to use an article before all nouns.
- A sentence with a long series of items is clearer when the article stands only before the first noun in the series.
- When you use the article in a series, make sure adjectives do not cause ambiguity: the article before the first item only means "new" applies to all items; an article before each item means "new" applies only to the first.
- A definite article is incorrect before a noun when an alphanumeric identifier comes after it (the identifier shows it is a proper noun).
- If unsure about articles, refer to a comprehensive grammar book, for example "Practical English Usage" by Michael Swan.

Examples:
- Non-STE: Turn shaft assembly. / STE: Turn the shaft assembly.
- Non-STE: Data module tells you how to operate unit. / STE: This data module tells you how to operate the unit.
- STE: You can use equivalent alternatives for these items.
- STE (no article, general): Solvents can cause damage to paint. / The machine operates with high efficiency. / This software increases performance.
- STE (short sentence): Install the nuts (2) and the bolts (3).
- STE (long series): Discard the O-rings (3), gaskets (4), seals (7), and washers (9).
- STE: Install the new O-rings (15), spacers (14), nut (13), and safety pin (12). (All items are new.)
- STE: Install the new O-rings (15), the spacers (14), the nut (13), and the safety pin (12). (Only the O-rings (15) are new.)
- Incorrect: Tag the circuit breaker 36L7. / CORRECT: Tag circuit breaker 36L7.

Application to software prose (interpretation): Write "Set the timeout in the config file", not "Set timeout in config file". Identifiers: "Run job 4711", "Open MR 123" (no article before a name plus number). General statements stay without articles ("Retries can hide errors").

## Section 5 - Procedural writing

Source: ASD-STE100 Issue 9 (2025-01-15), Part 1 - Writing rules, pages 1-5-1 to 1-5-7.
"Application to software prose" lines are interpretation for this skill, not document text.

### Summary of the rules (verbatim)

Sentences
- Rule 5.1: Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 20 words in each sentence.
- Rule 5.2: Write only one instruction in each sentence unless two or more actions occur at the same time.

Verbs in procedures
- Rule 5.3: Write instructions in the imperative (command) form.

Descriptive statements in instructions
- Rule 5.4: When there is a condition that the reader must know about first, start the instruction with a descriptive statement. Then, divide that descriptive statement from the command with a comma.

Notes
- Rule 5.5: Write notes only to give information, not instructions.

### Rule 5.1 Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 20 words in each sentence.

- Procedures give instructions that tell how to do a task. Long sentences in work steps are not easy to understand.
- In STE the maximum length of a sentence in a procedure is 20 words.
- Warnings, cautions and other safety instructions must also obey this rule.
- Notes (rule 5.5) do not obey rule 5.1. Notes are important in procedures, but they contain information only. Thus, the maximum length of a sentence in a note is 25 words.
- In the last STE example, the long sentence is divided into two shorter sentences, which are easier to read. You cannot divide this instruction into two different work steps, because the two actions occur at the same time.
- Section 8 gives all the rules about word count. (Not in these pages.)
- Counting, as shown in the examples (inference from the document's counts): reference numbers such as (2) count as words. A value with its unit ("6 mm", "(0.24 in)", "5 cc/minute") counts as one word. A two-word port name ("CROSS FEED") counts as two words: the rule 5.5 example sentence has 22 words only on that count.

Examples:
- STE: Install the three auxiliary screws (2) in the flange of the motor assembly (9). (14 words)
- STE: CAUTION: WHEN YOU REMOVE THE SHROUD (26), BE CAREFUL NOT TO CAUSE DAMAGE TO THE SURFACE OF THE FLANGE ASSEMBLY (22). (20 words)
- Non-STE: Put preservation oil into the unit through the vent hole until the oil level is approximately 6 mm (0.24 inches) below the surface of the flange cover. (25 words)
- STE: Put preservation oil into the unit through the vent hole. (10 words) Continue until the oil level is approximately 6 mm (0.24 in) below the surface of the flange cover. (16 words)

Application to software prose (interpretation): Instructions in a README, runbook or reply stay at 20 words or fewer, including identifiers and warnings. Split long steps into two sentences in the same step.

### Rule 5.2 Write only one instruction in each sentence unless two or more actions occur at the same time.

- Too many instructions in a sentence make it hard to read.
- Write one instruction in each sentence and show the sequence of the work steps clearly (usually with numbers or letters). You can use as many work steps as you want.
- You can write more than one instruction in a sentence when two actions occur at the same time.
- You can write more than one sentence in a work step: (a) when actions occur at the same time, (b) when a result occurs immediately after an action.

Examples:
- STE: (1) Set the TEST switch to the middle position. (2) Make sure that all the switches on the control panel are OFF. (Two work steps)
- Non-STE: Set the TEST switch to the middle position and release the SHORT-CIRCUIT TEST switch. / STE: A. Set the TEST switch to the middle position. B. Release the SHORT-CIRCUIT TEST switch. (Two steps: the actions do not occur at the same time.)
- STE, same time: Hold the panel in its open position and install the fastener. / Slowly extend the rod fully and make sure that it does not touch other parts. / Cut and remove the wire. / Remove and discard the seal.
- STE, several sentences in one step: Make sure that the locking torque of each of the four bolts (6) is a minimum of 0.30 Nm. Then, torque each of the four bolts (6) to 4.20 Nm. (The torque action immediately follows the check in one action.)
- STE, result after action: Measure the leakage from the outlet port. The leakage must not be more than 0.5 cc/minute. (The second sentence gives the limit for the result; one action.)

Application to software prose (interpretation): One command or action per sentence and per numbered step. "Run the build and push" is two steps. "Open and read the file" is one action in two verbs, so it is allowed. An expected result may follow its step in the same step.

### Rule 5.3 Write instructions in the imperative (command) form.

- An instruction tells the reader to do something. Write the verb in the imperative (command) form.
- Other sentence structures cause ambiguity. The reader will not know: if it is important to do a work step; if a different person did the work step; if a different person must do the work step in the future.
- Do not use the verb "must" before the imperative form, unless the instruction is very important for safety (for example in a safety instruction) or when you give an important condition.

Examples:
- STE: Set the switch to ON. / Remove the four bolts. / Increase the pressure to 60 psi. / Inflate the tires. / Install the new O-ring.
- Non-STE: The test can be continued. / STE: Continue the test.
- Non-STE: Oil and grease are to be removed with a degreasing agent. / STE: Remove oil and grease with a degreasing agent.
- Do not write: Before you remove the clamp, you must disconnect the hose. / WRITE: Before you remove the clamp, disconnect the hose.
- STE: WARNING: IF YOU MUST CUT THE WIRE, ALWAYS USE A PROTECTIVE MASK. PIECES OF WIRES CAN CAUSE INJURY.

Application to software prose (interpretation): Steps in a reply or runbook are commands: "Restart the service." Not "The service should be restarted." Commit subjects use the imperative already (conventional commits). Reserve "must" for safety or a key condition.

### Rule 5.4 When there is a condition that the reader must know about first, start the instruction with a descriptive statement. Then, divide that descriptive statement from the command with a comma.

- If a special condition is necessary for a work step, the reader must know it first. Write the condition first, then a comma to end the condition and start the instruction.
- The comma is important. Its position can change the meaning of the sentence.

Examples:
- Do not write: Set the switch to NORMAL when the light comes on. / WRITE: When the light comes on, set the switch to NORMAL.
- Do not write: Apply the primer when the surface is dry. / WRITE: When the surface is dry, apply the primer.
- Do not write: Disconnect the Constant Speed Drive (CSD) from the gearbox, if it does not operate correctly. / WRITE: If the Constant Speed Drive (CSD) does not operate correctly, disconnect it from the gearbox.
- Comma changes meaning: "If the CSD does not operate correctly, disconnect it from the gearbox." (the adverb modifies "operate") versus "If the CSD does not operate, correctly disconnect it from the gearbox." (the adverb modifies "disconnect"). Both are correct, the meanings differ.

Application to software prose (interpretation): "If the pipeline fails, rerun the job." Not "Rerun the job if the pipeline fails." The rule covers a condition the reader must know first, not every conditional. Check the comma.

### Rule 5.5 Write notes only to give information, not instructions.

- Notes give information to help the reader during a procedure. They contain descriptive text and obey the rules for descriptive writing.
- Notes must not give instructions, requirements or limits.
- A note can have one or more sentences. Each sentence has a maximum of 25 words.
- Do not use the imperative form in a note. With an imperative, the note becomes an instruction for a work step: make it a numbered work step.
- If you include instructions in a note, it is possible that the reader will not see the information. If the information in a note is important to prevent damage or injury, you must give it in a safety instruction (see the CAUTION and WARNING examples below; the hatch example has no imperative form).
- Do not use a note for limits, tolerances or results of a work step. This information must come directly after the related action in the work step.
- Test for correct use of notes: read the procedure without the notes and make sure the reader can do it correctly. If important information is missing and it is in a note: remove it from the note, write it in a work step, put the new step in the right place in the procedure, and repeat the test until fully sure the reader can do the procedure without the notes. A satisfactory result of the test shows that the notes are used correctly.
- Notes are used in procedures. You can write notes in descriptions only if the notes are necessary for illustrations or tables that are parts of such descriptions.

Examples:
- STE: NOTE: The gyroscope will become stable after approximately 15 seconds. / NOTE: It is not necessary to remove the nameplate (33) from the flange (27). / NOTE: You can use equivalent alternatives for these items.
- STE: NOTE: During the subsequent test, you get the cracking pressure when the fuel flow from the CROSS FEED port is more than 5 cc/minute. (One sentence, 22 words.)
- STE: NOTE: The data collection is not completed. Thus, the statistics module can give incorrect results. (Two sentences, 6 and 8 words.)
- Non-STE: NOTE: Make sure that the avionics ventilation system continues to operate correctly. (Imperative: not a note.) / STE: (6) Make sure that the avionics ventilation system continues to operate correctly. (Work step 6.)
- Non-STE: NOTE: When you connect the lines, do not bend them too much. If you bend the lines too much, you can cause damage to them. / STE: CAUTION: WHEN YOU CONNECT THE LINES, DO NOT BEND THEM TOO MUCH. IF YOU BEND THE LINES TOO MUCH, YOU CAN CAUSE DAMAGE TO THEM.
- Non-STE: NOTE: Before closing the hatch, it is mandatory to ensure that no persons are in the crew rest compartment. When the hatch is closed, there is no airflow to the compartment and therefore there is a risk of suffocation. (Not a note: a safety instruction, though it has no imperative.) / STE: WARNING: BEFORE YOU CLOSE THE HATCH, MAKE SURE THAT NO PERSONS ARE IN THE CREW REST COMPARTMENT. WHEN THE HATCH IS CLOSED, THERE IS NO AIRFLOW TO THE COMPARTMENT AND THERE IS A RISK OF SUFFOCATION.
- Non-STE: B. Measure the leakage from the outlet port. NOTE: The leakage must not be more than 0.5 cc/minute. / STE: B. Measure the leakage from the outlet port. The leakage must not be more than 0.5 cc/minute.

Application to software prose (interpretation): A "Note:" line in an MR or runbook states a fact ("The job takes about 15 minutes."). A requirement, limit or step goes into the numbered step. A risk of damage or data loss goes into an explicit warning that comes with the step. Test: the steps must work without the notes.

### Content structure (general text of the section)

- Descriptive writing gives information, not instructions. Thus, the imperative form of the verb is not permitted.
- Descriptive writing can be:
  - A description of an item, a product, a system, or a component, its function, how it is made and how it operates
  - A text that gives general information (for example, reports, brochures, and papers)
  - A note in a procedure.
- Application to software prose (interpretation): chat replies, MR descriptions and code comments that explain how something works are descriptive text. In them, write statements, not commands ("The job retries three times", not "Retry three times"). Imperative is for procedure steps.

---

### Rule 6.1 Give information gradually.

- In a descriptive text, give information gradually and make sure that each sentence contains only one subject.
- If you give too much information too quickly, the text is not easy to understand and the reader must read it again.
- The document's STE example uses short sentences, one subject in each. It is divided into two paragraphs with a logical structure: the first paragraph has five sentences, the second has two.
- Example (verbatim):

```
Non-STE:  Instrument Landing System
          During the approach to the runway, deviation pointers in the course indicators give
          commands to fly up or down and left or right. This information comes from the VHF
          transceivers, which are part of the Instrument Landing System. This helps the pilot
          during the landing approach. When the pilot responds to the commands, the
          aircraft can be flown over the runway centerline (localizer) and at a fixed angle
          (glideslope) to the runway threshold. The localizer signals are processed by the
          transceiver and data are transmitted to Air Traffic Control.

STE:      Instrument Landing System
```

### Rule 6.2 Use key words and key phrases to give your text a logical structure.

- Key words are words that occur in a text to connect different ideas. Key phrases are phrases that have the same function.
- These key words and key phrases show how information in a text is related and give the text a logical structure.
- You can also use connecting words and connecting phrases. They work like traffic signs and tell the reader if the information is new, different, or a result of previous information.
  - Approved connecting words (examples): "and," "but," "then," "thus"
  - Approved connecting phrases (examples): "as a result," "at the same time"
- When you use key words and key phrases, make sure that you do not change them in your text. The same terminology keeps the text clear and correct. If you use different terminology, the text is not easy to read.
- How the document's STE example connects (from the document):
  - Sentence 2 uses the key words "system," "show," and "pilot" again to add more information about sentence 1.
  - Sentences 3 and 4 use the key words "localizer course" and "glideslope path" again and add new information. They also include "runway," which refers back to sentence 1.
  - Sentence 5 connects to sentences 3 and 4 with "runway" again, then gives more information about the function of the indicators.
  - Key words and key phrases (for example, "show(s) the pilot") connect sentences 2 and 5.
  - Sentences 1 and 5 are also connected to make a logical paragraph.
  - Sentence 5 reuses the key phrase "during the approach to the runway" from sentence 1 to refer to the initial sentence and give more information about it. When you use the same key words and key phrases again, the sentences have a logical connection. If you use different terminology, the text is not easy to read.
  - There is also a logical connection between the two paragraphs. Paragraph 1 (sentences 1 thru 5): deviation, data, localizer, glideslope. Paragraph 2 (sentences 6 and 7): "This data about deviations from the localizer course and glideslope path comes from two VHF transceivers. These transceivers transmit this data to Air Traffic Control."
- Example pair (verbatim): see the 6.1 text. Sentence 1 "The Instrument Landing System (the system) on the aircraft shows data that helps the pilot during the approach to the runway." is followed by sentence 2 "This system shows the pilot the deviations from the localizer course and the glideslope path." The repeated words are the link.
- Application to software prose (interpretation): this is the same-term rule for a whole text. Name a thing once ("the worker"), then use that name each time. Do not switch to "the job", "the process" and "the daemon" for the same thing. Start the next sentence with the word the last sentence ended on.

### Rule 6.3 Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 25 words in each sentence.

- Good technical writing uses short sentences for complex topics. Short sentences give a clear structure and make information easier to understand.
- In descriptive writing, the maximum sentence length is 25 words. This is because descriptive text is more complex than procedural text.
- Examples (verbatim):

```
STE:      During the approach to the runway, deviation pointers in the course indicators
          show the pilot in which direction the aircraft must go.           (22 words)

Non-STE:  A smartphone is a cellular telephone that has an integrated computer and many
          other functions, such as an operating system, internet browsing as well as the
          ability to run software applications.                              (31 words)
STE:      A smartphone is a cellular telephone that has an integrated computer and many
          other functions. (15 words) It includes an operating system and an internet
          browser, and it can also operate software applications. (16 words)
```

- Application to software prose (interpretation): count words in each sentence of an explanation. Split at "such as ..." lists and at "which ..." clauses.

### Rule 6.4 Use paragraphs to show related information.

- In procedures, work steps usually have numbers and letters to show their sequence. In descriptive writing, paragraphs keep related information together and give a logical sequence to the text.
- In STE, a paragraph starts with a "topic sentence" which tells the reader about the topic of that paragraph. Then, the sentences that follow the topic sentence explain it or give more information related to it.
- In the document's example, the topic sentence at the start of each paragraph helps the reader understand the content of the paragraph and its topic.
- When a new paragraph starts, the reader knows that there will be a new topic or different information.
- Example (verbatim). The document says it has three short paragraphs, "which give the text a clear structure and make it easy for the reader to understand."

```
STE:  1. Lightning Strike
         A. General
           (1) A lightning strike can cause damage to the aircraft. The damage
               usually occurs at the attachment points. The attachment points
               include:
               - The nose of the fuselage
               - The tail section
               - The propellers
               - The wing tips
               - The tips of the stabilizer
               - The trailing edge of the stabilizer
               - The antennas
               - The static dischargers.
           (2) From the attachment points, the lightning strike usually goes aft
               through the structure of the aircraft. The high current of the lightning
               strike can cause damage to the mechanical systems of the aircraft.
               Thus, a permanent or a temporary malfunction of the electrical and
               electronic equipment and systems can occur.
           (3) If a lightning strike occurs, it is necessary to do an inspection of the
               aircraft for damage. The maintenance personnel must do this
               inspection before the next flight.
```

- Application to software prose (interpretation): in an MR description or a long chat reply, open each paragraph with a sentence that names its topic. A reader who reads only the first sentences gets the outline.

### Rule 6.5 Make sure that each paragraph has only one topic.

- In descriptive writing, paragraphs describe topics. Each paragraph has only one topic.
- The topic sentence is the first and most important sentence in a paragraph. It gives new information and makes a logical connection between the new information and previous information. To make this connection, it usually contains a key word and/or a connecting word or connecting phrase.
- From the topic sentences, the reader understands the contents of the text and finds the applicable information quickly. If the reader writes down each topic sentence, they get a good outline of the text.
- The other sentences in the paragraph give the information a logical structure and add more information on the topic of the paragraph.
- In the document's example, the topic sentence at the start of each paragraph helps the reader understand the content that the paragraph explains. More information then follows gradually and connects correctly to the information in the text.
- In the document's Instrument Landing System text (see rule 6.1), there are two paragraphs:
  - Paragraph 1 (sentences 1 thru 5): the topic is "Data to help the pilot."
  - Paragraph 2 (sentences 6 and 7): the topic is "How this data is transmitted."
- Example: the STE text under rule 6.1 is repeated in the document as the example for this rule. It is not repeated here.
- Application to software prose (interpretation): in an MR description, keep "what changed", "why" and "how to test" in separate paragraphs. Do not mix them.

---

- Paragraphs divide a text into logical units and help keep the reader's attention. If paragraphs are too long, they cannot do this.
- Do not put different topics in the same paragraph.
- If a paragraph has more than six sentences, divide it into two smaller paragraphs. This structure makes the text easier to read.

## Section 7 - Safety instructions

### Summary of the rules (verbatim)

| Group | Rule | Text |
|---|---|---|
| Definitions | | |
| How to write safety instructions | Rule 7.1 | Use an applicable word (for example, "warning" or "caution") to identify the level of risk. |
| How to write safety instructions | Rule 7.2 | Start a safety instruction with a clear and accurate command or condition. |
| How to write safety instructions | Rule 7.3 | Give an explanation to show the risk or possible result. |

### Definitions (general text of the section)

- Safety instructions tell the readers that procedures or work steps in procedures can be dangerous or cause damage.
- Usually, descriptive writing does not include safety instructions, unless these instructions are quoted text, or a descriptive text that includes safety risks.
- The words and definitions below agree with the specifications for technical publications that are applicable to aerospace and defense:
  - A warning tells the reader that there is a risk of injury or death.
  - A caution tells the reader that there is a risk of damage to objects.
- Note in the document: other industries or domains can use different words or categories for safety instructions. If you use different words (for example, "danger," "attention," or "notice") or graphical symbols, always make sure that the content obeys rules 7.1 thru 7.3. For more information, the document refers to:
  - ISO 45001:2018 (Occupational health and safety management systems, 2018)
  - ANSI Z535 series (2024)
  - ISO 3864 (Graphical symbols, Safety colours and safety signs, Parts 1 thru 4, 2024)
- Application to software prose (interpretation): the aerospace pair maps to "risk to people" and "risk to data or systems". In a software text, a warning marks a risk of data loss, an outage or a security breach. A caution marks a risk of a wasted run or a broken build. Use one fixed word for each level.

---

### Rule 7.1 Use an applicable word (for example, "warning" or "caution") to identify the level of risk.

- Use a word (for example, "warning" or "caution") or, when applicable, a symbol, to immediately show the reader the level of the related risk.
- Do an accurate risk analysis to identify the level of risk. Then decide:
  - If there is a risk of injury or death, use a "warning."
  - If there is a risk of damage to machines, tools, or equipment, use a "caution."
  - If there are the two levels of risk together, use a "warning."
- All examples in the section are in uppercase letters, but STE does not give rules for formatting. Refer to the applicable specifications for technical publications, style guides, and other official directives.
- Explanation of the oxygen example: the non-STE instruction is a caution. But oxygen mixed with other materials can cause explosions. Because there is a risk of injury or death, it must be a warning. The non-STE sentence is abstract and makes only a general statement. The STE warning gives clear and correct information about how to decrease the risk. It contains the words "explosion," "injury," and "death" so that the reader clearly understands how important it is.
- Examples in STE (verbatim):

```
WARNING: BEFORE YOU FILL THE LIQUID OXYGEN SYSTEM, PUT ON A FACE MASK AND
         PROTECTIVE CLOTHING. LIQUID OXYGEN CAN CAUSE IRRITATION OF THE
         RESPIRATORY TRACT AND EYE IRRITATION.

WARNING: ALWAYS KEEP YOUR HANDS AND FEET AWAY FROM THE BLADE. WHEN THE
         MOTOR OPERATES, THE BLADE TURNS AND CAN CAUSE INJURY.

CAUTION: BEFORE YOU OPERATE THE GROUND TEST UNIT, MAKE SURE THAT THE
         PRESSURE REGULATOR IS SET TO ZERO. THIS PRECAUTION WILL PREVENT
         DAMAGE TO THE UNIT.

CAUTION: DO NOT USE BLEACH OR CLEANSERS THAT CONTAIN CHLORINE TO CLEAN
         THE UNIT. THESE CLEANING AGENTS CAN CAUSE CORROSION.
```

- Examples, Non-STE and STE (verbatim). The document gives them after the risk analysis and the three-level decision list above:

```
Non-STE: CAUTION: EXTREME CLEANLINESS OF OXYGEN TUBES IS IMPERATIVE.

STE:     WARNING: MAKE SURE THAT THE OXYGEN TUBES ARE FULLY CLEAN.
                  OXYGEN AND GREASE MAKE AN EXPLOSIVE MIXTURE. AN
                  EXPLOSION CAN CAUSE INJURY OR DEATH.
```

- Application to software prose (interpretation): put the level word first on the line ("WARNING:" or "CAUTION:"). When both kinds of risk apply, use the higher level. Name the concrete risk, not a general statement.

---

### Rule 7.2 Start a safety instruction with a clear and accurate command or condition.

- The reader must know how to prevent accidents and keep a high level of safety.
- Start with a clear and accurate command.
- If the reader must know about a condition before the start of a procedure or work step, give this condition first.
- Examples (verbatim, in STE):

```
Examples in STE (refer to the command at the start):
WARNING: DO NOT SWALLOW THE SOLVENT. ALWAYS MAKE SURE THAT YOU KNOW
         THE SAFETY PRECAUTIONS AND FIRST AID INSTRUCTIONS FOR SOLVENTS.
         SOLVENTS ARE POISONOUS AND CAN CAUSE INJURY OR DEATH.

CAUTION: DO NOT USE BLEACH OR CLEANSERS THAT CONTAIN CHLORINE TO CLEAN
         THE UNIT. THESE CLEANING AGENTS CAN CAUSE CORROSION.

Examples in STE (refer to the condition at the start):
WARNING: WHILE YOU USE THE SPRAY PAINT, POINT THE SPRAY AWAY FROM YOUR
         FACE. THE SPRAY PAINT CAN CAUSE INJURY TO YOUR EYES.

CAUTION: WHEN YOU ASSEMBLE THE UNIT, DO NOT LET THE PARTS FALL.
         IF THEY FALL, PERMANENT DAMAGE TO THE PARTS CAN OCCUR.
```

- Application to software prose (interpretation): start a warning with the verb ("Do not run this on production.") or with the condition when the order matters ("Before you run the migration, stop the worker.").

---

### Rule 7.3 Give an explanation to show the risk or possible result.

- If it is possible, always tell the reader about the problems that can occur if the reader does not obey the safety instruction.
- If there is a clear and specified risk, the person who does the task understands the risk and is more careful.
- The examples in the document are the same four as in rule 7.2. The last sentence of each is the explanation (risk or possible result):
  - "SOLVENTS ARE POISONOUS AND CAN CAUSE INJURY OR DEATH."
  - "THESE CLEANING AGENTS CAN CAUSE CORROSION."
  - "THE SPRAY PAINT CAN CAUSE INJURY TO YOUR EYES."
  - "IF THEY FALL, PERMANENT DAMAGE TO THE PARTS CAN OCCUR."
- Application to software prose (interpretation): end every warning with the result: "The migration deletes all rows in the table." Say what breaks, not "this is dangerous".

### Rule 8.1 You can use all standard English punctuation marks but not the semicolon (;).

- The semicolon is not permitted "because it lets you write very long sentences. It is also not easy to use correctly."
- Alternative: "always write two different sentences."
- Examples (verbatim):
  - Non-STE: (1) Examine the removed parts; replace the damaged ones.
  - STE: (1) Examine the removed parts for damage. (2) Replace the damaged part(s).
  - Non-STE: The battery is not user-replaceable; it can only be replaced by an approved service station.
  - STE: Users cannot replace the battery. Only specialists at approved service stations can replace it.
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Never write a semicolon in prose. Split into two sentences. Semicolons inside code, shell commands and quoted text are not prose and stay.

### Rule 8.2 Use hyphens (-) to connect words that are directly related.

- A hyphen "connects words or parts of words." Use it "for technical nouns to show that two or more words are directly related. This construction helps the reader to understand words and phrases more easily."
- Five cases, with the document's examples:
  1. Terms that have two or more words and are adjectives before a noun: low-altitude flight, high-pressure chamber, air-conditioned compartment, transmitter-receiver system, quick-release fastener, clamshell-type flap, eighteen-inch monitor, cast-aluminum bracket, three-to-one ratio, trial-and-error method, air-to-air refueling, soap-and-water solution, up-to-date information, run-on torque, break-away torque, cut-in speed, in-flight entertainment system, stiff-bristled brush, fire-resistant material, self-sealing hose.
  2. Two-word fractions or numbers: forty-seven, ninety-ninth, one hundred and sixty-two, three-sixteenths, one thirty-second.
  3. Terms that contain an uppercase letter plus a noun, or a number plus a noun, and that usually give the shape or configuration of something: L-shaped bracket, O-ring, T-shirt, U-beam, Y-coupling, V-band clamp, 3-prong connector, 180-grit abrasive cloth.
  4. Verbs that contain a noun or a different part of speech as the first part: die-cast, arc-weld, fusion-bond, stop-drill, vacuum-pack, heat-treat, jump-start, air-condition, short-circuit, fast-forward, cold-roll, dry-clean, blow-dry.
  5. Terms in which the end of the prefix is a vowel, and the root word starts with a vowel: pre-amplifier, de-icing, anti-icing, pre-engage.
- Note in the document: "A hyphen is different from a dash, which divides ideas, shows a range, or gives a signal for a pause. A dash is usually longer than a hyphen, but it is at times shown as a hyphen with a space on each side."
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Hyphenate a multi-word modifier before a noun ("read-only mode", "post-merge check", "up-to-date branch"). Do not use a dash to join clauses.

### Rule 8.3 You can use parentheses:
- To make references to illustrations or text
- To include letters or numbers that identify items on an illustration or in a text
- To identify the work steps in a procedure
- To include abbreviations
- To give the singular and plural forms of a noun at the same time
- To explain words or a part of a sentence
- To include an alternative.

- Seven permitted uses. Examples (verbatim):
  1. References to illustrations or text: "Remove the valve (10, Figure 1)." / "Install the cover (refer to paragraphs 2 thru 5)."
  2. Letters or numbers that identify items: "Disconnect the hoses (2) and (12) from the suction ejector (8)." / "Remove the nuts (74), the washers (76), the bolts (68), the seals (70), and the bonding straps (72)."
  3. Work steps in a procedure: "(1) Install the locking cap (4) on the body (8). (2) Safety the locking cap (4) with the cotter pin (5). (3) Install a new retaining ring (6)."
  4. Abbreviations: "A Liquid Crystal Display (LCD) is a flat-panel display that uses the light-modulating properties of liquid crystals."
  5. Singular and plural at the same time: "A. Before you do the test(s), install the component(s)." / "B. Do the applicable test(s)."
  6. Explain words or a part of a sentence: "Increase the pressure slowly (not more than 10 psi each minute)." / "Make sure that the BLEED pushbutton switch is released (the ON legend is off)."
  7. Alternative: "Open the left (right) access panel L42 (R42)."
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Parentheses are allowed for an abbreviation, a reference such as (see `docs/x.md`), an item ID such as (#123), or a short explanation. The text inside is its own sentence for the word count (Rule 8.5), so keep it short.

### Rule 8.4 In a vertical list, a colon (:) has the same effect on word count as a period and shows the end of a sentence.

- The colon "divides the first part of the sentence from the subsequent items in the vertical list. This colon has the effect of a period (full stop)."
  - Procedural sentences: maximum 20 words before the colon.
  - Descriptive sentences: maximum 25 words before the colon.
- "Each item in a vertical list that comes after the colon counts as a new sentence." Limit for each item: 20 words (procedural), 25 words (descriptive).
- Example (verbatim):
  - STE: "To extinguish a possible fire, portable fire extinguishers are installed in these areas:" (13 words)
    - The cockpit (2 words)
    - The cabin (2 words)
    - The cabin sub-compartment (3 words)
    - The crew rest compartment. (4 words)
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Lead-in sentence before a bullet list is counted alone, up to the limit. Each bullet is counted as its own sentence. A bullet can be a fragment, as in the example.

### Rule 8.5 When you put text in parentheses, it counts as one word in that sentence.

- "The words that you put between parentheses also make a new sentence. Thus, count them in that different sentence."
- An identifier in parentheses (a number, a letter, or an alphanumeric identifier) counts as one word in the sentence. Abbreviations in parentheses also count as one word.
- Examples (verbatim):
  - "Make sure that the EMER pushbutton switch is released (the EMER legend is off)." (This sentence has 10 words, because the text in parentheses counts as one word. The sentence in parentheses has 5 words and counts as a different sentence.)
  - "Remove the safety pin (10)." (5 words)
  - "Installation of a Business Class (B/C) Seat" (7 words)
  - "Hardware and Software Configuration Check of the In-Flight Entertainment (IFE) System" (11 words)
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. A parenthetical note does not hide words. Count it as one word in the host sentence and also as a separate sentence of its own.

### Rule 8.6 Count each of these elements as one word:
- Numbers
- Numbers together with units of measurement
- Abbreviations
- Alphanumeric identifiers
- Quoted text
- Titles, headings, and text on placards and labels
- Proper nouns of individuals, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities.

(The summary table words the last-but-two item as "Titles, headings, placards, and labels".)

- This is the word count for "sentence length". Seven cases:
  1. Numbers. "Do steps 13 thru 16 a minimum of three times." (10 words; "13" and "16" are numbers, one word each.) "The spar box has twenty-one ribs." (6 words; "Twenty-one" is one word.) "Do not count numbers that identify paragraphs or work steps. These numbers are usually part of the document numbering systems."
  2. Numbers together with units of measurement. "Units of measurement usually follow their related number (for example, 10 mA). When you write a number together with its unit of measurement, count them as one word." Examples: "Make sure that the temperature in the room is 10 °C." (10 words) = "Make sure that the temperature in the room is 10 degrees Celsius." (10 words). "The unit weighs 20 kg." (4 words) = "The unit weighs 20 kilograms." (4 words). "The resistance must be 10 [ohm symbol]" (5 words) = "The resistance must be 10 ohms." (5 words). (The ohm symbol is lost in the PDF text of the source.)
  3. Abbreviations. "In technical texts, you can use abbreviations (also acronyms and initialisms) to make sentences shorter and easier to read. But these abbreviations only help the reader if the reader knows their meanings. Each abbreviation counts as one word." Examples: "The maintenance team does a test of this system each day at 10 a.m." (13 words; "a.m." counts as one word together with its number.) "During this safety check, obey NASA protocols." (7 words; acronym.) "For remote access, use the VPN." (6 words; initialism.)
  4. Alphanumeric identifiers. "A combination of letters and numbers that identifies something. Each alphanumeric identifier counts as one word." "Examine the No. 1 bearing installation." (5 words) and "Tag circuit breaker 36L7." (4 words).
  5. Quoted text. "Usually given in quotation marks. Words between quotation marks count as one word in a sentence. In some sentences, or parts of the document, uppercase letters or differences in font can also show quoted text. Such text also counts as one word." Examples: "Touch the "Service Overview" arrow to select the function page." (9 words) / "Release the SHORT-CIRCUIT TEST switch." (4 words) / "C = (A - B) - 0.063 mm" (1 word; "In STE, a formula is quoted text and counts as one word.")
  6. Titles, headings, and text on placards and labels. These are "words or groups of words that you cannot change": titles and headings; text on placards (for example, a warning notice in a work area or public place); text on labels (for example, warning and caution labels attached to objects). Examples: "Before you start a repair, refer to the Structural Repair Manual for the applicable safety procedures and precautions." (16 words) / "Refer to Testing and Fault Isolation, page block 1001." (6 words) / "Refer to Requirements after Job Completion for the applicable procedures." (7 words) / "This procedure is for the inspection of SSI No. 57-21-16, "Outer wing bottom skin lower surface spanwise skin joints at stringer 13 and stringer 20 between Rib 12 and Rib 27 excluding areas covered by flap track fairings 3, 4, and 5."" (9 words: 7 words, plus the reference number as one word, plus the quoted SSI title as one word, which "is not in STE, and it is not possible to change it") / a quoted placard text ("Interior hazards exist to such a degree that interior operations may be conducted only after full examination, and with extreme caution.") counts as 1 word / a quoted shipping label ("FRAGILE - Please handle with care.") counts as 1 word.
  7. Proper nouns of individuals, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities. "Groups of words that you cannot change because they identify" individuals (John Smith), groups or organizations (World Health Organization), geopolitical entities (Republic of Ireland). Examples: "The first president of the United States of America was George Washington." (8 words) / "The owner of STE is the Aerospace, Security and Defence Industries Association of Europe." (7 words).
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. In my replies and commit text, code spans (`foo_bar`), file paths, identifiers such as ticket IDs, versions with units, quoted error messages and product or repo names would each count as one word. The unchangeable quoted text may be non-STE. Do not use this to hide ordinary words in quotation marks: only text that "you cannot change" qualifies.

### Rule 8.7 Hyphenated words count as one word.

- "Groups of words that are not usually adjectives but have the function of an adjective before a noun are hyphenated. Such groups of words count as one word."
- "When you use hyphens in long technical nouns to make them clearer to the reader, a hyphenated group of words also counts as one word."
- Examples (verbatim): "Clean the surface with a soap-and-water solution." (7 words) / "Use the trial-and-error method." (4 words) / "Cutoff-switch power connection" (3 words) / "Main-gear-door retraction-winch handle" (3 words).
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. A real hyphenated term counts as one word. A hyphenated group of long technical nouns can be used to make a long noun phrase readable, as in the last two examples.

### Rule 9.1 Use a different sentence construction to write a sentence when a word-for-word replacement is not sufficient.

- "STE is a controlled natural language with a controlled dictionary." The dictionary gives approved alternatives for words that are not approved. If an alternative has the same part of speech, you can use it as a word-for-word replacement. Example: Non-STE "A value of 2 mm is acceptable." ("Acceptable" is not approved.) STE "A value of 2 mm is permitted."
- "When you replace a word, always make sure that the alternative that you select does not change the meaning of the sentence. If the meaning changes, or if the alternative does not have the same part of speech, you must use a different sentence construction."
- A different construction is necessary in four cases:
  1. You must change the grammatical structure to use the alternative. Usually the STE examples show how.
     - Non-STE: The oil level on the sight gauge must be visible during the test.
     - STE: During the test, make sure that you can see the oil level on the sight gauge. (The verb "see" replaces the adjective "visible", so "must be" becomes "make sure that you can".)
     - Non-STE: Cycle the unit twice to remove air from the lines.
     - STE: Operate the unit for two cycles to remove air from the lines. (Noun "cycle" with verb "operate" replaces the verb "cycle"; the technical noun "two" replaces the adverb "twice".)
     - "When you use different sentence constructions, always make sure that you use approved words that do not change the meaning of the sentence."
  2. A word-for-word replacement gives a meaningless result.
     - Non-STE: Without this modification, the service life of the unit can be uncertain. ("Uncertain" is not in the dictionary; "not sure" and "not known" are possible.)
     - Do not write: "...the service life of the unit cannot be sure." or "...cannot be known." ("These sentences are not clear, and they are not good English. You must think about the meaning of the sentence and then use a different sentence construction.")
     - STE: Without this modification, it is possible that the service life of this unit will be shorter than usual.
     - "Always make sure that you know the purpose of your communication and that the words that you use give the meaning that you want."
  3. The approved alternative changes the meaning.
     - Non-STE: Just apply very light pressure to the surface.
     - STE: Only apply very light pressure to the surface.
     - NOT: Immediately apply very light pressure to the surface. ("Immediately" is the approved alternative for "just" but changes the meaning here.)
  4. The word that you must replace is not in the dictionary.
     - Non-STE: The incidence of water in fuel is dangerous.
     - STE: Water in fuel is dangerous. ("Incidence" is not in the dictionary. A word with the same meaning is "presence", but "presence" is not approved and its approved alternative is "be". So use a different sentence construction that keeps the same meaning.)
- How to do it: "When you cannot do a word-for-word replacement, use different words to get the same result."
  - Non-STE: Lift the seat so that it clears the track locks.
  - STE: Lift the seat until it is away from the track locks.
  - NOT: Lift the seat so that it cleans the track locks. ("clear" is not an approved verb and its only dictionary alternative is "clean", which has the wrong meaning. "Do not use "clear" as a verb or think that "clear" can be a technical verb.")
  - "Clears the track locks" is the result of the previous action "lift the seat". The action that is necessary is to increase the distance between the seat and the track locks. So tell the reader "lift the seat" (the action) "until the seat is away from the track locks."
  - Questions to answer before you write: "What is the meaning of the word 'clear' in this context?" and "What is the action that a reader must do?" If you are not sure, find the applicable definition in an English dictionary.
- "Frequently, you must select different words, use different verb forms, or write new sentence constructions. It is possible that you must divide long sentences into shorter ones, remove information that is not necessary, or get more information from an engineer. Frequently, you must make many changes to your text. Then, when you change one part of a text, you must make sure that it has no negative effects on other parts of the text."
- Worked example (crack table):
  - Non-STE: "If cracks are detected during this procedure, the operator must perform the repair within a certain number of flight hours depending on crack length. Refer to following table:" followed by a table headed "Crack length detected" / "Time before repair", with the rows "L> ..." 1000 flight hours, "L = ..." 2000 flight hours, "L< ..." 3000 flight hours.
  - STE: "If you find cracks, refer to the table that follows:" followed by a table headed "If the crack is of this length" / "Do the repair before", with the same three rows (1000, 2000 and 3000 flight hours).
  - Faults in the non-STE sentence: words not approved; "are detected" is passive voice; the first sentence is 24 words; an article is missing before "crack length" and "following table"; the instruction is not in the imperative form.
  - Dictionary replacements: "perform" to "do", "within" to "in", "certain" to "some", "detect" to "find". With the missing articles added, the sentence becomes "If cracks are found during this procedure, the operator must do the repair in some flight hours depending on the crack length. Refer to the following table:". It keeps the same construction, but it is not good English.
  - To replace "are found" (passive voice), change the construction to "If you find cracks ...". This does not change the construction of the rest of the sentence. To replace "following", make a small change: "Refer to the table that follows:". To replace "the operator must do the repair", change the construction to "do the repair". To replace "in some flight hours", use "number of flight hours". To replace "depending", make an important change to the sentence, because a word-for-word replacement with the dictionary alternative "if (conj)" given for "depend" is not possible.
  - The intermediate attempt "If you find cracks during this procedure, do the repair before the number of flight hours that are applicable to the crack length." has 23 words, "the technical meaning is not correct and not easy to understand", and repeats table information. "A good solution is to put the instruction in the table heading."
- "The primary objective of STE is that readers immediately understand each sentence that they read."
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. When a plain synonym swap makes an odd or wrong sentence, rewrite the sentence around the action. Split long sentences, drop information the reader does not need, and put repeated information in one place (a table or list heading).

### Rule 9.2 Use each approved word correctly.

- Some approved words have a meaning "applicable only in some contexts (restricted meaning)". "Before you use a word, read its definition in the approved meaning column of the dictionary." In STE "approved words usually only have one approved meaning. Other meanings that the word can have in standard English are not approved." "Always make sure that the word that you select has the correct meaning in the applicable context."
- Meaning examples (verbatim):
  - Non-STE: Wear protective clothing. / STE: Use (or put on) protective clothing. ("Wear" is approved as a verb with the meaning "to become damaged by friction.")
  - Non-STE: This regulation extends to all units. / STE: This regulation is applicable to all units. ("Extend" has the approved meaning "to increase, or cause something to increase, in dimension or range." In the Non-STE example it does not agree with that meaning.)
  - Non-STE: When the pressure goes down, lift the cover. / STE: When the pressure decreases, lift the cover. ("Goes" with "down" refers to physical movement, for example a gauge needle or flag. "Decrease" refers to the pressure, not to the indicator.)
  - Non-STE: Make sure that the probe went through more than 500 °C. / STE: Make sure that the temperature of the probe increased to more than 500 °C. ("go" with "through" only for something that physically moves through something, not for a requirement or condition.)
  - Non-STE: Move the tube to see if the inner connection is tight. / STE: Move the tube to make sure that the inner connection is tight. ("See" only for something you can see with your eyes, not for "come to know".)
  - Non-STE: The indicator turns green. / STE: The color of the indicator changes to green. ("Turn" only means "to move or cause to move around an axis or a point.")
  - Non-STE: Do not let the pressure go below (or above) 20 psi. / STE: Do not let the pressure become less than (or more than) 20 psi. ("Above" and "below" are for physical positions only and not for limits.)
- Part of speech: "an approved word can usually only have one function (part of speech). In STE, use each approved word as the approved part of speech." Reason: English words usually have no forms that show their function, so readers can think differently about the same word.
  - Non-STE: When you work with cleaning agents, use breathing equipment. / STE: When you do work with cleaning agents, use breathing equipment. ("Work" is approved as a noun, not as a verb.)
  - Non-STE: Install the cover with the help of a second person. / STE: Install the cover with the aid of a second person. ("Help" is approved as a verb, not as a noun.)
  - Non-STE: Be careful not to damage the sleeve. / STE: Be careful not to cause damage to the sleeve. ("Damage" is approved as a noun, not as a verb.)
- Exception: "There are a small number of words that are approved as more than one part of speech and have more than one meaning. These words are important and frequently occur in technical English." Example "flush": verb ("to remove something or to operate with a flow of liquid"): "Flush the pipes with a disinfectant solution." Adjective (one surface fully touches another): "Make sure that the door is flush with the adjacent surfaces." "Because the verb and the adjective have different positions and contexts, it is easy to see their function."
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Use a word in one sense and one part of speech. Do not turn a noun into a verb or a verb into a noun on your own ("cause damage", not "damage" as a verb; "the aid of", not "the help of"). Check the dictionary first.

### Rule 9.3 When you use two words together, do not make phrasal verbs.

- A phrasal verb is "a verb and one or more prepositions" with "a meaning that is different from the meanings of its parts." Phrasal verbs usually have a concrete and an abstract meaning. General example: "Put out the cat." (concrete: put the cat outside of the house) / "Put out the fire." (abstract: extinguish the fire).
- "To prevent ambiguity, it is not permitted in STE to use approved words together to make a new phrase (phrasal verb)."
- Examples (verbatim):
  - Non-STE: After you put out the fire, close the valve on the fire extinguisher. / STE: After you extinguish the fire, close the valve on the fire extinguisher. ("Extinguish" means "to stop burning" and is the most usual word in technical documentation.)
  - Non-STE: This compound can give off poisonous fumes. / STE: This compound can release poisonous fumes. ("Release" means "to make free," or "to let go.")
- "You will not usually find phrasal verbs listed as 'not approved' in the dictionary. When you write a standard English sentence in STE, always make sure that the new sentence is grammatically correct. And make sure that you use the approved words with the meaning that they have in the dictionary."
- Exception: "Only a small number of phrasal verbs (for example, "put on" and "come on") are approved in the dictionary. They all have a restricted meaning."
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Replace "spin up", "roll back", "pull in", "bump", "kick off", "carve out" with a single plain verb ("start", "restore", "include", "increase"). Check the approved phrasal verbs in the dictionary section before using one.

### Rule 9.4 When you select terminology or wording, always use a consistent style.

- "In procedures, you will frequently give the same information again and again... When you select terminology or wording for a work step, use the same terminology or wording each time that type of work step occurs. The reader will identify the terminology or wording and will quickly understand the action. Different terminology or wording can cause confusion and delays."
- Two sentences can each be correct STE and give the same instruction: "Lubricate the two bolts with a small quantity of oil." and "Apply a small quantity of oil to the threads of the two bolts." If both appear in different work steps for the same instruction, "you do not help the reader. Select one sentence or sentence style and then use it again and again when the context (and meaning) is the same."
- Example (verbatim, condensed to the differences). Non-STE:
  1. Put the housing (8) in its installation position on the main body (9).
  2. Lubricate the two bolts (10) with oil.
  3. Install the two bolts (10) in the body (9).
  4. Torque-tighten the two bolts to 4 Nm.
  5. Put the transducer (11) on the body assembly (9).
  6. Apply a small quantity of oil to the threads of the four bolts (12).
  7. Attach the transducer (11) to the body assembly (9) with the four bolts (12).
  8. Torque each of the four bolts (12) to 0.8 Nm.
  STE:
  1. Put the housing (8) in its installation position on the body assembly (9).
  2. Apply a small quantity of oil to the threads of the two bolts (10).
  3. Install the two bolts (10) in the body assembly (9).
  4. Torque each of the two bolts to 4 Nm.
  5. through 8. unchanged.
- The non-STE text has three inconsistencies: different terms for item (9) ("main body", "body", "body assembly"); different wordings for tightening ("torque" and "torque-tighten"); different sentences for lubrication ("lubricate the XX bolts" and "apply a small quantity of oil to the threads of the XX bolts").
- In the STE text "each time that the same item occurs, it has the same noun, and the same context always has the same wording." In procedures the reader reads one work step at a time.
- Descriptive writing: "the reader must understand the text as a unit. Thus, it is important to use words and sentences correctly and give a logical structure to the text."
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Pick one name per thing (for example "merge request", not "MR", "PR" and "pull request" together) and one sentence pattern per repeated action in a procedure or in a list of commit-body steps. The document states the consistency rule for procedures, and a logical structure for descriptive text.

### General recommendations (GR)

"The general recommendations (GR) in this section are not STE rules. They can help you prevent typical errors that writers make."

### GR-1 The conjunction "that"

- Use "that" to connect a subordinate clause to a main clause, for example after verbs such as "make sure," "show," and "recommend."
- Native speakers often leave "that" out, but "this conjunction helps the reader understand where the end of the main clause and the start of the subordinate clause is. Thus, use the conjunction "that" as much as possible to prevent ambiguity." It also helps translation because in many other languages the equivalent word cannot be omitted.
- Examples (verbatim):
  - Do not write: Make sure the valve is open. / WRITE: Make sure that the valve is open.
  - Do not write: The manufacturer recommends you prepare the mixture in an area with good airflow. / WRITE: The manufacturer recommends that you prepare the mixture in an area with good airflow.
  - Do not write: The gauge shows the reservoir is full. / WRITE: The gauge shows that the reservoir is full.
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Write "Make sure that the test passes", "The log shows that the job failed". Never drop "that" after "make sure", "show", "recommend", "verify", "check", "ensure".

### GR-2 The preposition "with"

- "With" has three approved meanings: "association or relationship," "help or sharing," or "a means or instrument". It can cause ambiguity. "Install the panel with the green fasteners" can mean: install the panel that has green fasteners; install the panel together with the green fasteners; use the green fasteners to install the panel.
- Usually the context is enough. But "when you use the word "with," read your sentence again. Make sure that your sentence does not cause ambiguity, that there are no errors, and that the meaning of the sentence does not change."
- Examples (verbatim):
  - Do not write: Lift the aircraft at the maximum takeoff weight with passengers. / WRITE: Lift the aircraft at the maximum takeoff weight (passenger weight included). (Two meanings are possible, and one is a joke.)
  - Do not write: Make sure that the lever does not touch the stop (1) with hydraulic pressure supplied. / WRITE: When you supply hydraulic pressure, make sure that the lever does not touch the stop (1). ("Write the condition first.")
  - Do not write: Use tool TS9867 to seal the opening. / WRITE: Seal the opening with tool TS9867. ("This sentence is clear because it gives the tool number. In STE, you must give the primary action verb which is "seal" and not "use."")
- When you replace "with" by another construction, "make sure that you show the primary action verb in the work step."
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Re-read each "with" and ask which of the three meanings is intended. Put the condition first. Lead with the real action verb ("Seal the opening with X", "Run the tests with the flag").

### GR-3 How to use pronouns

- Pronouns (for example "it," "they," "that," "these," "those") refer to a person, location or item already in the text. If you use them correctly, the text is easy to read. "In STE, the approved pronouns are in the dictionary. Do not use a pronoun if it is not in the dictionary (for example, "she" or "he")."
- "If a pronoun can refer to one or more nouns in a text, it can cause ambiguity in a sentence. If there is ambiguity, replace the pronoun with the word that it refers to."
- Example (verbatim):
  - Do not write: If you engage the pins incorrectly with the seats, they can become damaged.
  - WRITE: If you engage the pins incorrectly with the seats, the pins can become damaged. / ... the seats can become damaged. / ... the pins and seats can become damaged.
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. If "it" can mean two things (a service and a config), repeat the noun.

### GR-4 The pronoun "this"

- "Make sure that the reader knows the item the pronoun refers to. If "this" can refer to more than one item, give the applicable context again."
- Examples (verbatim):
  - Do not write: Make sure that the cover is not locked (this can cause damage to the probe). (Which is the cause of damage: the locked cover or the unlocked cover?) / WRITE: Make sure that the cover is not locked. If the cover is locked, this can cause damage to the probe. / Or: If the cover is locked, damage to the probe can occur.
  - Do not write: Do not use crocus cloth on aluminum parts. If you do this, you can cause corrosion on aluminum parts. Crocus cloth contains ferrous oxide. / WRITE: Do not use crocus cloth on aluminum parts. Crocus cloth contains ferrous oxide, which can cause corrosion on aluminum parts.
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Do not start a sentence with a bare "This". Say "This change", "This error", or restate the cause.

### GR-5 False friends

- A false friend "looks the same as one in a person's native language but that has a different meaning in a different language" (English "disposition" vs Italian "disposizione" or Spanish "disposición"). Non-native writers must check that the word has the correct English meaning.
- Example (verbatim):
  - Do not write: Obey the dispositions of the manufacturer when you use this adhesive. ("disposition" in English does not mean an "instruction.")
  - WRITE: When you use this adhesive, obey the manufacturer's instructions.
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Check words such as "actual", "eventually" and "sensible" against their English meaning, not against a look-alike word in your own language.

### GR-6 Latin abbreviations

- Latin abbreviations ("e.g." for "for example," "i.e.," for "that is," "etc." for "and so on"): "STE recommends that you do not use Latin abbreviations because they can confuse your readers if they do not know them. Always use English words to make the text clear."
- Examples (verbatim):
  - Do not write: Discard the standard parts (e.g., washers, screws, bolts, and nuts) each time you remove them. / WRITE: Discard the standard parts (for example, washers, bolts, and nuts) each time you remove them.
  - Do not write: These wires can have insulation of different colors (blue, green, red, etc.). / WRITE: These wires can have insulation of different colors. They can be blue, green, red, or other colors.
  - "You can omit these abbreviations when they are not necessary." WRITE: These wires can have insulation of different colors.
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Write "for example", "that is", "and so on" or "or other X". Do not write "e.g.", "i.e.", "etc.", "vs.", "via" or "cf." in prose.

### GR-7 Inclusive language

- "Inclusive language prevents bias and makes sure that all persons have respect and representation. The objective of inclusive language is to use neutral and non-discriminatory terms and expressions to promote equality and inclusivity in communication."
- "STE does not include examples of inclusive language, but it fully complies with gender-neutral language requirements. When you write in STE, make sure that you always use gender-neutral language. Gender-specific pronouns, for example "he" or "she" are not permitted in STE. The words "man" and "woman" are also not permitted in STE, unless these words are necessary in an applicable context, for example, in a medical text. STE always uses neutral terms and constructions to prevent gender bias in technical documentation."
- References named: European Institute for Gender Equality (2019), Toolkit on Gender-sensitive Communication; United Nations (2019), Disability-inclusive communications guidelines. Also "international, national, company, or industry-specific guidance documents".
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Use "they", "the user", "the author" or the role. Name a person only if needed.

### GR-8 Possessive form

- The possessive (Saxon genitive) adds an apostrophe and "s", for example "refer to the manufacturer's instructions."
- "STE is a controlled natural language that emphasizes clear writing and simplicity." "While the possessive form is permitted in STE, make sure that you use it correctly. If you are not sure that your sentence is correct, do not use the possessive form." Other languages lack it or use another form, so it is not always easy for non-native readers.
- "Always refer to technical publication specifications, style guides, or other official directives for information and rules."
- Application to software prose: Interpretation. Allowed. Use it only for clear cases ("the manufacturer's instructions"). Prefer "the config of the service" or "the service config" when unsure.
